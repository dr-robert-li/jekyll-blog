# Evidence packet G07

- **Domain:** epidemic simulation for public health policy
- **What the model does:** an individual-based simulation of disease transmission that projects infections, deaths and hospital/intensive-care demand under different non-pharmaceutical interventions (school closure, case isolation, household quarantine, social distancing); its outputs were given to the national government's scientific advisory structures.
- **Scale:** tens of millions of residents were subject to the restrictions the outputs informed.
- **Adoption:** A+0 = a modelling report produced with [MODEL] by [OPERATOR] (a university research team) was submitted to the national scientific advisory group and published, and was reported as informing the national government's shift to population-wide restrictions.
- **De-identification:** model, operator, people, courts, committees, statutes and places tokenised; not fully feasible (the case is widely known).

## Context (not episodes)
- [A+0] Press reports said the single modelling report from [OPERATOR] had persuaded the government to change its approach; the national scientific advisory group's first published evidence list showed that report as the only listed piece of modelling on interventions (S4).
- [A+0] [OPERATOR] did not at first publish the code; a restructured (refactored) version of the code was later released publicly. Informal criticism of the code's quality circulated online and in the press (S2, S3).
- [A+0] Commentators and some scientists argued in the press and online that the model's assumptions were pessimistic or that its no-intervention death projections were too high; a senior epidemiologist said the school-closure result in the [ORG-1] reanalysis applied to a specific, probably unrealistic, scenario (S1).
- [A+5.9, after window] The national statistics regulator wrote to the [INQUIRY-1] about how the inquiry's report summarised modelling estimates; outside the period of this packet and not counted.

## Episodes

E1. **[A+0.2] Independent computational reproduction (open code-checking certification).**
- Brought by: a university neuroscientist acting for [ORG-2], an open code-checking initiative. Who requested the check: not reported. Funded or run by: [ORG-2]; computation run on [ORG-3] (a university) high-performance computer.
- Sought: to check whether the published code and scripts reproduce the figures and tables in the adoption-year report.
- Outcome: certificate of reproducible computation issued. The reviewer re-ran all the simulations and found the key results reproducible; small differences (mostly under 5%) were observed in the numbers between the report and the re-runs. Output was found to be the same across operating systems and compilers for a fixed thread count and random seed.
- Stated reasons: the check concerned whether the computation could be reproduced; it expressly did not review the epidemiological assumptions or whether the model was correct.
- Relief applied to: none (verification only); the certificate applied to the specific report.
- Sources: S2, S3.

E2. **[A+0.6] Peer-reviewed independent reanalysis in a general medical journal.**
- Brought by: four researchers at [ORG-1] (a university), physics and astronomy department. Funded or run by: not reported.
- Sought: to re-run [MODEL] using the publicly released code, to recreate what policy-makers could have known at adoption, and to test the adoption-year projections against subsequent data.
- Outcome: published. The authors reproduced the qualitative results of the adoption-year report. They found that the model, if started with a higher reproduction number (about 3.5), would have given a good forecast of the subsequent data, including peak intensive-care demand. They also reproduced the adoption-year finding that school closure lowered transmission but, in the simulation, increased total deaths over the epidemic when no vaccine was available, and found that distancing measures applied to the over-70s reduced deaths more than population-wide distancing. Final death tolls depended mainly on the age profile of those infected.
- Stated reasons: model behaviour under the interventions as simulated; the authors said the long-run death count depended on who was infected rather than how many.
- Relief applied to: none; the operator described the paper as independent confirmation of its modelling.
- Sources: S1, S5.

E3. **[A+0.7] Judicial review of the national restriction regulations, permission stage at [COURT-2 (appellate)].**
- Brought by: [PLAINTIFF-1] (a businessman) and two co-claimants. Funded or run by: not reported in the judgment; private solicitors acted for the claimants.
- Sought: declarations that the restriction regulations made under [STATUTE-1] (public-health statute) were unlawful, on three grounds: lack of statutory power; ordinary public-law grounds (fettering, failure to consider relevant matters, irrationality); and breach of human-rights provisions. Under the public-law ground the claimants argued that the minister had to take into account the unreliability of the evidence from the [OPERATOR] modelling team.
- Outcome: [COURT-1] (first instance) refused permission. [COURT-2 (appellate)] granted permission on the statutory-power ground only and dismissed that ground on its merits; permission was refused on the public-law and human-rights grounds as academic (the regulations had been replaced) and not properly arguable.
- Stated reasons: the minister had power to make the regulations. On the evidence point the court said the argument had no evidential foundation; the minister had taken and had access to expert advice, and the fact that others disagreed with some of those expert views did not make the decision unlawful.
- Relief applied to: none.
- Sources: S6.

E4. **[A+0.8] Lower-house parliamentary select committee inquiry (science and technology) on the use of scientific advice.**
- Brought by: [COMMITTEE-1], the lower-house science and technology select committee, on its own initiative; it took oral evidence from the head of [OPERATOR]'s team on three occasions, including a session dedicated to mathematical modelling.
- Sought: to examine how scientific advice, including modelling, informed the government's pandemic response.
- Outcome: report published with conclusions and recommendations. The committee found that public understanding and scrutiny in the early weeks were hampered by lack of transparency: when details of the adoption-year report were published, it was not known what other evidence the advisory group and government were considering. It said published modelling is uncertain and should not be read as a strict prediction; that modelling expertise had initially dominated the advisory group, which it judged a fair reflection of the lack of data early on; and that analysis chosen for public presentation must meet high standards of rigour (in connection with a later projection graph criticised by the statistics regulator). Government published a response.
- Stated reasons: transparency of the evidence base, the uncertainty in modelling, and the breadth of expertise.
- Relief applied to: recommendations addressed to government and its advisers generally.
- Sources: S4.

E5. **[A+1.6] Joint lower-house select committee inquiry (health and social care; science and technology) on lessons from the initial response.**
- Brought by: [COMMITTEE-2] and [COMMITTEE-1] jointly, on their own initiative; evidence from over 50 witnesses and over 400 written submissions.
- Sought: to examine preparedness, non-pharmaceutical interventions (including the timing of national restrictions), testing, social care and vaccines.
- Outcome: report with 38 recommendations; government published a response at A+2.3. The committees found that the early approach in practice accepted widespread infection as inevitable and called this a serious early error; that agreement between advisers and government showed a degree of groupthink; that full national restrictions were inevitable and should have come sooner; and that the scientific-advice structures lacked transparency, international representation and structured challenge.
- Stated reasons: scrutiny of the timing of decisions and the way scientific advice, including modelling, was produced and challenged. Whether the report examined [MODEL] specifically was not verified (see notes).
- Relief applied to: recommendations to government and public bodies generally.
- Sources: S7, S8.

E6. **[A+5.7] Statutory national public inquiry, module on core political decision-making ([INQUIRY-1]).**
- Brought by: [INQUIRY-1], a statutory inquiry set up by the government and chaired by a retired senior judge. Funded or run by: government-funded statutory inquiry.
- Sought: to examine core decision-making and political governance across the four national governments, including the use of scientific and modelling advice and the timing of national restrictions. The head of [OPERATOR]'s team gave written and oral evidence on the operator's models.
- Outcome: report published with 19 recommendations. The inquiry found the response was often insufficient and came too slowly; that the national restrictions saved lives but only became inevitable because of earlier acts and omissions of all four governments. Its executive summary, relying on evidence from the head of [OPERATOR]'s team and a related academic modelling paper, stated that mandatory restrictions a week earlier would have reduced first-wave deaths in one nation by about half (tens of thousands of fewer deaths).
- Stated reasons: the inquiry's assessment of the full body of evidence on decision timing; modelling outputs were used to estimate the effect of earlier action.
- Relief applied to: recommendations to governments generally (decision-making structures, communication, parliamentary scrutiny of emergency powers).
- Sources: S9, S10, S11.
