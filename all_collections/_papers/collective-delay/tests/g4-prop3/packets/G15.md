# Evidence packet G15

- **Domain:** public health-insurance disability services (individual annual budgets for adults with developmental disabilities living at home or in the community)
- **What the model does:** a statistical (regression-type) spreadsheet tool that takes scores from a needs inventory and a standardised adaptive-behaviour assessment, completed by contracted assessors, and calculates each participant's annual dollar budget for community services.
- **Scale:** thousands of participants (roughly three to four thousand adults).
- **Adoption:** A+0 = [AGENCY-1] put into use the budget tool at issue, re-estimated from earlier data with new weights and variables (including "living situation") and a much lower constant; budgets for many participants fell later that year. Earlier budget-calculation tools had existed before this version.
- **De-identification:** model, agency, state, courts, statutes, firms and people tokenised; partly feasible.

## Context (not episodes)
- [A-0.3] A new state statute ([STATUTE-1]) required that any budget increase above the tool's figure be justified by a participant's "health and safety" need; the term was left undefined (S3).
- [A+0] Notices of reduced budgets gave no reason beyond a statement that the inventory and case file had been used (S2).
- [A+0.5] [AGENCY-1] refused to disclose the formula, calling it a trade secret; the copyright holder of the adaptive-behaviour assessment opposed release of its booklet (S3, S5).
- [A+8.3] [AGENCY-1] reported to a state disability council that it was redesigning services in parallel with the court-supervised tool replacement (S7).

## Episodes

E1. **[A+0 onward] Individual administrative reconsiderations, reviews and fair hearings.**
- Brought by: individual participants (or guardians) dissatisfied with their calculated budgets. Funded or run by: participants, often helped by unpaid service coordinators; [AGENCY-1] did not fund advocates for appeals.
- Sought: higher individual budgets.
- Outcome: before [AGENCY-1] abolished its human reconsideration step, about three-fifths of budgets reviewed were increased. Thereafter the first step was an informal file review by one of two programme managers, who could raise a budget only on a "health and safety" showing; denials gave no reasons. A further "exception review" was open only to about two-thirds of participants. Hearing officers could only uphold or send back decisions, refused to order release of the assessment booklet, and the agency's director and then state court were the further levels. Aggregate numbers of hearings and their outcomes are otherwise not reported.
- Stated reasons: not reported in aggregate.
- Relief applied to: individual appellants only.
- Sources: S3.

E2. **[A+0.5 to A+13.7] Federal class action, [COURT-1], with appeal to [COURT-2 (appellate)].**
- Brought by: adult participants (initially twelve, later sixteen named representatives) suing the head of [AGENCY-1]; a near-identical second suit by other participants was consolidated with it at A+1.8. Funded or run by: [ORG-1], a civil liberties organisation.
- Sought: restoration of budgets; adequate notice of the reasons for cuts; disclosure of the formula and data; a fair hearing before reductions; a lawful budget method; individual claims that cuts put the named plaintiffs at risk of institutionalisation.
- Outcome:
  - A+0.6–A+0.7: the court blocked the reductions; an agreed preliminary injunction restored budgets to their pre-adoption levels and barred cuts until the agency sent court-approved notices and made the documents used to calculate budgets available for copying.
  - A+1.1 and later: the court twice rejected the agency's proposed budget notices as failing to explain reductions; certified a statewide class action of all participants and applicants and extended the injunction to all of them.
  - A+3.9: [COURT-2] affirmed the extension of the injunction to all participants, holding that participants have a property interest in their benefits and that notices which did not say why budgets fell were inadequate; it declined to review the notice ruling for lack of jurisdiction.
  - A+4.7: on summary judgment, the court held that the tool's unreliability arbitrarily deprived participants of their property and violated due process. It found the agency had discarded about two-thirds of the original records used to build the tool (data-entry errors, unmatched IDs, missing assessment and claims data), that one region was under-represented, that the agency's own staff estimated the tool gave inadequate budgets to 10–15% of participants (hundreds of people), and that the agency had never re-estimated or tested the tool as intended (on legal advice during the litigation). It also held that due process required written standards defining "health and safety", reasons for denying informal review, a committed suitable representative before a reduction could be confirmed, and access to the assessment booklet pages needed to contest a score. It ordered the agency to prepare plans, with plaintiffs' counsel, to improve and regularly test the tool.
  - A+5.2–A+5.5: the parties settled; the court approved the settlement after a fairness hearing. The agency agreed to develop and implement a new budget tool with an outside consultant and participant input, within a target of about two years, while "bridge" protections let participants keep the higher of their old or new budgets. The court kept jurisdiction to enforce.
  - A+9.4: after the target passed, the court set a completion deadline about eighteen months later and awarded fees to plaintiffs' counsel. The deadline was missed.
  - A+12.2: a federal magistrate judge ruled that barring participants from all access to information about the replacement assessment instrument was unconstitutional; the instrument's publisher then withdrew, and the agency had to start choosing a tool again.
  - A+13.3: the court appointed a special master to help select and implement a new tool.
  - A+13.7: the court held the agency in civil contempt for failing to implement a new tool, rejecting its impossibility defence and finding it had not taken every reasonable step (including a unilateral delay linked to federal funding conditions); it ordered payment of plaintiffs' counsel's monitoring fees for one year and refused other requested changes. By the end of the window no replacement tool had been implemented.
- Stated reasons: as above (notice inadequate; tool unreliable and untested; appeal route without standards, reasons, representation or access to scoring materials).
- Relief applied to: all participants in and applicants to the programme statewide (thousands); the original preliminary relief first applied to the named plaintiffs.
- Sources: S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6.
