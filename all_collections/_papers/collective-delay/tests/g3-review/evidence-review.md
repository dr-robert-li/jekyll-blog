# G3 evidence review: prior tests of P3, P14 and P23

Scope: which existing experiments and surveys already test, partly test, or complicate predictions P3 (Part F), P14 (G.4) and P23 (H.5) of `q2-collective-delay-research.md`. Research used web search and publisher, PubMed and repository pages only. No datasets were downloaded. Tags follow the report's convention:

- [admit: source] means the claim was confirmed this session against the source named.
- [refute: source] means an authoritative source contradicts the claim, and the correction is given.
- [abstain: reason] means the claim is unverifiable, there is a source-vs-prior conflict, the source is non-authoritative, or the confidence is unearned at this evidence tier.

Where only an abstract or an indexed summary was reached, the tag says so. Several full texts could not be opened in this session: the fetch tool returned empty pages, and ScienceDirect refused access. Those claims rest on abstracts.

### Verdict: mixed by domain, and partly falsified as stated

- **The mechanism is established.** Correcting misperceived support changes behaviour when three conditions hold:
  - the barrier is social sanction or conditional cooperation (3.1, 3.24);
  - the act is cheap or a first step (3.1, 3.2, 3.10, 3.11, 3.13);
  - the target sits below the true support level and is told about *support* rather than *prevalence* (3.2, 3.19).
- **"More than harm information" has only weak head-to-head support.** Nolan et al. 2008 (3.11) is the only direct comparison found, and it concerns a cheap behaviour. In tax compliance, information about consequences worked too (3.14). Andre et al. and Bursztyn et al. had no harm-information arm.
- **"Across domains" fails for costly behaviours and in health:**
  - pensions backfired (3.15);
  - actual climate donations did not move (3.6, 3.8);
  - college drinking campaigns had no meaningful effect (3.17);
  - the health meta-analysis is null after bias correction (3.18).
- **Boomerangs are documented** for targets already above the norm (3.12), for targets shown a lower local norm (3.9), for people who had overestimated support (3.8), and for upward comparison when catching up is costly (3.15).
- **The sign of \(\delta\) is not fixed.** B.4 calibrates \(\delta > 0\) on the 26-pp *support* gap, but the decision rule applies it to \(r\), the share *acting*. For rare costly acts, people overestimate \(r\) (3.6), so \(\delta < 0\), and "true" information *lowers* \(\hat r\). That is a problem with how the model is specified, not just a moderator.

### Moderators identified

1. **Costly vs cheap behaviour.** Supported for cheap or first-step acts (Opower, door-hangers, the job-matching sign-up, a lottery-share donation). Null or reversed for costly, repeated or rare acts (actual climate donations, retirement saving, binge drinking).
2. **Target's position relative to the norm.** Works on those below the true support level or above a harmful norm (high prescribers, high energy users). Backfires on those better than the norm (Schultz), on those who had overestimated support (JEBO 2026, though not in Andre et al.), and in low-support reference groups (Goerg et al.).
3. **What is corrected.** Information about *support* (injunctive) beats information about *prevalence* (descriptive) in Andre et al. and in Rhodes et al. Tiede shows that the *prevalence* of rare acts is overestimated, so correcting prevalence pushes the wrong way.
4. **Whether the barrier is social.** The Saudi and xenophobia cases involve a visible act that others sanction. Pensions and donations are private, so there is no sanction for correction to remove.
5. **Publication bias.** The strongest syntheses (3.17, 3.18) shrink or remove the average effect.

### Open question new work must answer

Does information about true private *support*, as distinct from the prevalence of the behaviour, change *actual* costly behaviour more than equally vivid harm information, once behavioural cost and the target's prior position are crossed? This needs a preregistered factorial field experiment in at least two non-climate domains. Treatments: support information / harm information / both / control. Crossed with: cheap vs costly target behaviour. Analysed by: the respondent's prior relative to the truth. Outcome: actual behaviour. No study found does this.

## P14: attribution to the root cause decays along a causal chain

**Current text (G.4).** "Public and elite attribution to the root cause declines with each link between the root and the harm." Model: \(\varphi_k \approx \varphi_0\prod a_j + M\).

### Verdict: mixed, with established components and an untested form

**Already established:**
- Responsibility is *traceability*-dependent, and legislators anticipate it (Arnold 1990; Mildenberger & Sahn 2025 for elites).
- In vignettes, blame of agents weakens with chain length (Engelmann & Waldmann 2022), mainly because intervening voluntary agents absorb it (Fincham & Shultz 1981).
- Voters attribute to visible decision points and to relief rather than prevention (Gasper & Reeves 2011; Healy & Malhotra 2009).
- Partisanship gates attribution (Malhotra & Kuo 2008; the 2025 fire polls), and media frames move its locus (Iyengar 1991; Boukes 2022).
- Attribution of an event to climate is what links experience to action (Ogunbode et al. 2019).

**What complicates P14 as written:**
1. **Decay is not uniform per link.** Physical links pass attribution through to a human root (Hilton et al. 2010). Decay concentrates where an intervening agent offers a substitute target (insurers in Pew 2026; Fincham & Shultz). So \(a_j\) depends on the type of link, not just on how many links there are.
2. **Elites and publics differ.** Mildenberger & Sahn found voters indifferent to traceability and legislators responsive. P14's "public and elite" should be split, and the elite half is better supported.
3. **\(a_j\) is partly made.** Blame avoidance (Hood 2011) and Arnold's link-breaking show that veto holders choose chain length. That ties P14 to V and P11.
4. **The shark-attack evidence is not robust** (Fowler & Hall 2018). Use droughts and disaster relief instead.

**What is new in P14:** the multiplicative form across *several* cross-domain links, with a *policy failure* at the root; measurement of the *same* harm at different chain depths; and the M term that resists decay. I found no study that varies chain depth beyond two links for a policy-root harm, in publics or elites.

### Open question new work must answer

Holding the harm fixed, does attribution to a policy root fall with each added link, and does it fall at agentive links but not at physical ones? Does the elite–public gap that Mildenberger & Sahn found for traceability hold for attribution itself? The design is a survey experiment that crosses depth (1–4), link type (physical vs intervening agent) and party cue. It would run in parallel on a public sample and on legislators or staff.

### Verdict: mixed. The individual half is partly supported; the institutional half is untested.

**Established:**
- Skills decay with disuse, and cognitive, accuracy-based skills decay faster than physical ones (Arthur et al. 1998).
- In automated cockpits, the cognitive skills of manual flight degrade more than stick skills (Casner et al. 2014), but fine-motor skills also erode when practice is low (Haslbeck & Hoermann 2016).
- Experts defer to a wrong model output, and experience protects only partly (Dratsch et al. 2023).
- Novices who practise with an unrestricted model perform worse unassisted (Bastani et al. 2025; Shen & Tamkin 2026, preprint).
- Organisational skill erosion under cognitive automation has been observed and went unnoticed (Rinta-Kahila et al. 2023).

**Not established:**
- That experienced practitioners' *existing* diagnostic skill falls *because* of AI delegation in clinical practice. The only study (Budzyń et al.) is observational, short and contested.
- The widely cited general claims: Sparrow et al.'s Experiment 1 failed to replicate; Kosmyna et al. is a non-peer-reviewed preprint; Gerlich is correlational.

**Refinements the evidence supports:**
1. **Separate three mechanisms.** P23 currently merges them:
   - automation bias while assisted (immediate);
   - atrophy of an existing skill through disuse;
   - failure to form the skill in novices trained with the model.
   The causal evidence is strongest for the third and weakest for the second.
2. **Expect a task gradient.** Diagnostic and monitoring tracing (cognitive, accuracy-based) decays faster than perceptual-motor execution, but motor skill is not immune.
3. **Engagement moderates.** The effect is smaller where users verify, ask for explanations or stay on task (Casner's mind-wandering result, Shen & Tamkin's engaged usage patterns, Lee et al.'s confidence effects, Bastani et al.'s guardrails).
4. **Measure the decay.** Rinta-Kahila et al. found that erosion goes unacknowledged, so the decline has to be measured by audits rather than self-report.

### Open question new work must answer

In practitioners who were already competent, does delegating diagnosis or tracing to a model *cause* a decline in *unassisted* proficiency over months to years? Is the decline larger for cognitive than for motor components, and does mandated unassisted practice prevent it? This needs a stepped-wedge cluster trial of model deployment with periodic unassisted probes, recording case mix and procedural covariates (such as withdrawal time), run for at least 12 months. The institutional question, whether such mandates are cut first under fiscal pressure, has no data at all.
