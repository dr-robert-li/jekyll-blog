# Evidence packet K30
Label: Substance class X, biocidal additives in hull paints for boats, introduced to replace a restricted predecessor
De-identification: de-identified
t0 (first credible early warning): 1993
Scoring point: t0 + 5 = 1998. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

Names replaced: [X] = the class of hull-paint biocides; [X1] = a triazine compound in the class; [X2] = a urea herbicide also used as an agricultural weedkiller; [X3] = a zinc compound also used in anti-dandruff shampoo; [Agent T] = the predecessor organotin antifoulant. "EEA case chapter" = EEA (2013) *Late lessons from early warnings* II, case chapter on [X].

## Evidence
Q1. [paraphrase] Preventing fouling organisms from growing on hulls is essential for shipping and boating. A 1 mm layer of algal slime increases hull friction by 80% and cuts ship speed by 15%, and fouling raises fuel use considerably. — Source: EEA case chapter, §12.1.1 (citing MER 1996); Date: 1996
Q2. [paraphrase] Antifouling paints are the most economically efficient solution yet found. Shortly after the Second World War, [Agent T] became a common additive, especially in "self-polishing" paints that are designed to release persistent toxic substances. — Source: EEA case chapter, §12.1.1–12.1.2; Date: 1940s–1980s
Q3. [paraphrase] [Agent T] caused shell malformations in oysters. In one French bay alone it caused an estimated USD 147 million loss of oyster production. — Source: EEA case chapter, §12.1.2 (citing Alzieu 1991); Date: 1991
Q4. [paraphrase] [Agent T] caused imposex, the development of male sexual organs in female sea snails. The effect was first noticed in the early 1970s but was attributed to [Agent T] only in the 1980s, after many snail populations had declined. Molluscs were affected below 10 ng/L. — Source: EEA case chapter, §12.1.2; Date: early 1970s–1980s
Q5. [paraphrase] A European Community directive banned [Agent T] on boats under 25 m, mainly pleasure craft. — Source: EEA case chapter, §12.1.2 and Table 12.4; Date: 1989
Q6. [paraphrase] After the restrictions on [Agent T], its concentrations in that French bay fell from 900 ng/L (1983) to below 10 ng/L by the late 1980s, and the oyster beds recovered. Some hailed the limited controls as a "solution". — Source: EEA case chapter, §12.1.3; Date: 1983–late 1980s
Q7. [paraphrase] New Zealand restricted [Agent T] for leisure craft in 1989 and banned it for New Zealand vessels in 1993. Imposex did not fall at one marina near a commercial wharf. — Source: EEA case chapter, Table 12.1 (citing Smith 1996); Date: 1989–1996
Q8. [paraphrase] [X] were developed, at least initially, for the small-boat market after the 1989 restriction, to boost the performance of copper-based paints. They were believed to be less damaging to aquatic life than [Agent T]. — Source: EEA case chapter, §12.2 and chapter summary; Date: 1989–1990s
Q9. [paraphrase] The compounds used or promoted included agrochemicals ([X2]), compounds already registered as biocides ([X1]) and personal-care ingredients ([X3]). — Source: EEA case chapter, §12.2; Date: 1990s
Q10. [paraphrase] In the United Kingdom during the 1990s, antifouling use was dominated by copper(I) oxide, followed in order by [X2], [X1], [X3] and one other organic biocide. — Source: EEA case chapter, §12.2; Date: 1990s
Q11. [paraphrase] The first report of [X] in coastal waters found [X1] at up to 1 700 ng/L around marinas on the French Riviera. It was discovered by chance during a survey of agricultural triazine herbicides, which share the same analytical protocol. — Source: EEA case chapter, §12.2 (citing Readman et al. 1993); Table 12.4; Date: 1993
Q12. [paraphrase] This study triggered further research that confirmed contamination of boating areas in several regions. — Source: EEA case chapter, §12.2; Date: 1993–1998
Q13. [paraphrase] A researcher sampling Bermuda's coastal waters found high [X1] levels in some harbour areas, raising fears of damage to the algae that live symbiotically in corals. — Source: EEA case chapter, Box 12.2 (citing Readman 1996); Date: 1995–1996
Q14. [paraphrase] The first study of [X1]'s toxicity at environmental levels detected long-term effects on periphyton (attached algal and microbial films) at 63–250 ng/L, within the range reported for coastal waters. — Source: EEA case chapter, §12.2–12.3.1 (citing Dahl & Blanck 1996); Table 12.4; Date: 1996
Q15. [paraphrase] Most of these biocides act herbicidally on photosynthesis (photosystem II), so marine plants and algae at the base of food chains are particularly vulnerable. Their effects are broader-spectrum than [Agent T]'s. — Source: EEA case chapter, §12.3.1 and §12.4.3; Date: 1996
Q16. [paraphrase] Some of the same compounds reach the sea from other sources, including agricultural and domestic weed and fungus control ([X2]) and anti-dandruff shampoos ([X3]). — Source: EEA case chapter, §12.2; Date: 1990s
Q17. [paraphrase] The earlier events prompted expanded environmental research on [X]. — Source: EEA case chapter, Table 12.4; Date: 1997
Q18. [paraphrase] Extreme seawater warming caused mass coral bleaching, with mortality above 90% to considerable depths in parts of the Indian Ocean. This left corals more vulnerable to further stress. — Source: EEA case chapter, §12.3.2 (citing Sheppard et al. 2002); Date: 1998
Q19. [paraphrase] Hull coatings have been needed since antiquity. Earlier coatings, such as lead sheathing, copper sheathing and copper, arsenic and mercury paints, caused "no or limited" environmental concern, until organotins caused increasing warnings from the late 1950s or early 1960s. — Source: EEA case chapter, Table 12.4; Date: antiquity–1960s
