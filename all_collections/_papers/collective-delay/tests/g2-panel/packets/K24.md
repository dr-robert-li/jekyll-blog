# Evidence packet K24

- **Label:** Monomer X, a gaseous chemical intermediate used to make a widely used plastic (Plastic Y).
- **De-identification:** Partly feasible. The substance, the plastic and the firms are given neutral labels. Coders with occupational-health knowledge may recognise the case and know the outcome; score only from the items below.
- **t0:** 1959. EEA (2013, ch. 8, Table 8.2): "Consultant tells [a producer] that [the monomer] is more toxic than believed and [another producer]'s toxicologist says 500 ppm limit would lead to 'appreciable' harm." This is the first dated warning after the earlier entry "1930s–1959: acute toxicity demonstrated".
- **Scoring point:** t0 + 5 = 1964. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

## Evidence

Q1. [paraphrase] Industrial output of Monomer X began in the 1930s, mainly to make Plastic Y. Products included shower curtains, food containers, floor coverings, pipes, packaging and wire coating. More than 95% of the monomer goes into Plastic Y. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.2; Date: 1930s–1950s.

Q2. [paraphrase] World production grew from 450 tonnes a year in 1933 to 54,000 tonnes during the Second World War, and to 145,000 tonnes by 1952. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.2, citing IARC 1974; Date: 1933–1952.

Q3. [paraphrase] Even after polymerisation into the plastic, residual unreacted monomer remains in the material. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.2; Date: general property of the material.

Q4. [paraphrase] In animal tests in the 1930s and 1940s, inhaling the monomer caused drowsiness, loss of coordination and loss of consciousness at 5,000–120,000 ppm, and congestion of the lungs, liver and kidneys at high concentrations. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.3.1, citing Patty et al. 1930, Peoples and Leake 1933, Oster et al. 1947; Date: 1930–1947.

Q5. [paraphrase] In humans, acute exposure caused giddiness, nausea and headaches. In one accidental poisoning in a plastics plant, workers died almost immediately after losing consciousness. Accidental spraying caused skin rash and second-degree burns. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.3.1, citing Peoples and Leake 1933, Harris 1953, Danzinger 1960; Date: 1933–1960.

Q6. [paraphrase] Scientists in one country reported hepatitis-like liver inflammation in 15 of 48 exposed workers. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.3.1, citing Tribukh et al. 1949; Date: 1949.

Q7. [paraphrase] Further studies in the same country's plastics factories found vascular disease (angioneuropathy) in workers exposed at levels as low as 5–15 ppm. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.3.1, citing Filatova and Gronsberg 1957; Date: 1957.

Q8. [paraphrase] The chemical manufacturers' trade association set the workplace threshold limit value at 500 ppm in 1954. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.1 and §8.3.2; Date: 1954.

Q9. [paraphrase] The 500 ppm limit rested on one small guinea-pig study from the 1930s, sponsored by a federal mining agency. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, Table 8.2 note 10, citing Markowitz and Rosner 2002, p. 172; Date: 1930s–1954.

Q10. [paraphrase] There was no federal workplace-safety regulator. Threshold limits were set by a volunteer body with no formal ties to government, whose members included government officials, academics and industry consultants. Its limits were often based on inadequate health information and reflected what industry felt achievable rather than what protected health. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.3.2, citing Castleman and Ziem 1988 and Markowitz and Rosner 2002 (describing the period before 1971); Date: 1954–1964.

Q11. [paraphrase] A 1958 federal food law prohibited any food additive shown to cause cancer in humans or animals. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.2 (the 1958 Delaney amendment); Date: 1958.

Q12. [paraphrase] Internal company documents show that in the 1950s industry already had animal data indicating that the workplace limit was too high. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.1, citing Markowitz and Rosner 2002; Date: 1950s.

Q13. [paraphrase] In May 1959 a toxicologist at Producer A wrote to Producer B that the 500 ppm limit would cause "appreciable injury" to full-time workers, and raised particular concern about long-term exposure. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.3.2, citing Rowe 1959; Date: 1959.

Q14. [paraphrase] In November 1959 a consultant wrote to the medical director of Producer C that, based on an "off-the-record phone call" from Producer A's toxicologist, the monomer was "more toxic than has been believed". He wrote: "Even 100 ppm produced organ weight changes and gross pathology." — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.3.2, quoting Smyth 1959; Date: 1959.

Q15. [paraphrase] The same letter reported that Producer A was considering whether the findings had "any bearing on the safety of (food) packaging uses". The consultant asked that the information stay confidential until Producer A published it. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.3.2, citing Smyth 1959; Date: 1959.

Q16. [paraphrase] Producer A's researchers published a study of rats, guinea pigs, rabbits and dogs exposed for six months. It reported liver abnormalities at 500 ppm, increased liver weight in rats at 100 ppm, and liver enlargement with degenerative changes in rabbits at 200 ppm. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.3.2, citing Torkelson et al. 1961; Date: 1961.

Q17. [paraphrase] By 1961 company toxicologists were recommending internally that exposure be no higher than 50 ppm. Producer A says it lowered its own internal limit to 50 ppm that year, but the limit is known to have been exceeded. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.1 and §8.3.3, citing Castleman and Ziem 1988; Date: 1961.

Q18. [paraphrase] A clinical study of 168 workers making Plastic Y was the first to describe the monomer's role in acroosteolysis. This is an extremely rare disease of excruciatingly painful bone resorption, with skin and vascular changes and enlargement of the liver and spleen. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.3.3, citing Suciu et al. 1963; Date: 1963.

Q19. [paraphrase] European Producer D observed acroosteolysis in workers at one of its factories, saw the same disease at another of its factories in a second country, and informed other producers. Several more cases appeared in its plants between 1962 and 1965. No reports were made public or sent to independent scientists, governments or workers. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.3.3, citing Maltoni 1974a; Date: 1962–1964.

Q20. [paraphrase] A physician doing routine medical checks for Producer C followed up a worker's complaint about painfully tender fingers. He found four cases of acroosteolysis among workers in the same area of the plant, which he took as obvious evidence of a link to workplace conditions. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.3.3, citing Markowitz and Rosner 2002, p. 173; Date: 1964.

Q21. [paraphrase] Producer C asked an industry-supported occupational-health laboratory to investigate. Its director concluded that acroosteolysis was an "entirely new" occupational disease. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.3.3, citing Markowitz and Rosner 2002; Date: 1964.

Q22. [paraphrase] Producer C's medical director asked a physician at another of its plants to "determine as quietly as possible whether similar conditions existed at his plant". He added: "We do not wish to have this discussed at all and I request you maintain this information in confidence." — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.3.3, quoting manufacturers' association papers, 12 November 1964 (Markowitz and Rosner 2002, p. 174); Date: 1964.

Q23. [paraphrase] Workers and the public were kept in the dark about the bone disease, while the information circulated among producers. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 8, §8.3.3; Date: 1962–1964.
