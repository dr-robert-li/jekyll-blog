# Evidence packet K12

- **Label:** Radiofrequency electromagnetic fields from handheld mobile and cordless phones, and possible brain tumour risk.
- **De-identification:** Not feasible. The technology is defined by its product and its users, and naming it is needed to make sense of the exposure evidence. Coders may know later developments. Score only from the items below.
- **t0:** 1999. EEA's chapter treats the first Swedish case-control study of mobile phone use and brain tumours (published 1999) as the starting early warning. EEA's own formal warning came later, in 2007.
- **Scoring point:** t0 + 5 = 2004. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

## Evidence

Q1. [paraphrase] Sweden was among the first countries to adopt wireless telephony widely. The analogue NMT 450 network ran from 1981, NMT 900 from 1986, and digital GSM (900/1 800 MHz) from 1991. Desktop cordless (DECT-type) phones were used from 1988. — Source: EEA 2013 *Late lessons II* ch. 21 §21.2; Date: 1981–1991

Q2. [paraphrase] Large increases in use, and therefore in exposure, came from the late 1990s. The brain is the main target organ when a handheld phone is held to the head. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 21 §21.1; Date: late 1990s

Q3. [paraphrase] A US lawsuit alleged that repeated mobile phone use had caused a fatal brain tumour in a woman. The *Los Angeles Times* headline read "Suit Over Cellular Radiation Raises Hazard Questions". — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 21 §21.2 (citing Carlo and Schram 2001); Date: 1993

Q4. [paraphrase] After the lawsuit, the US wireless industry funded a research programme, Wireless Technology Research, of about USD 25 million, led by a scientist chosen by the industry. Its director later described conflicts with the funders over the findings. — Source: Carlo and Schram, *Cell Phones: Invisible Hazards in the Wireless Age* (2001), cited in EEA 2013 ch. 21; Date: 1993–2001

Q5. [paraphrase] A Swedish committee review found an increased risk of childhood leukaemia with proximity to power lines (extremely-low-frequency fields), and a literature review suggested higher brain tumour risk in the electronics industry. This prompted the Swedish group to add mobile phone questions to its case-control studies. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 21 §21.1 (citing Hardell et al. 1995); Date: 1995

Q6. [paraphrase] The first Swedish case-control study (209 cases, 425 controls, about 90% response) found no overall association. It found a slightly raised, non-significant risk for analogue phone use with more than 10 years' latency (OR 1.20, 95% CI 0.56–2.59). For tumours in the temporal, occipital or temporoparietal areas on the side where the phone was held, the OR was 2.42 (95% CI 0.97–6.05). Numbers were low, "so no firm conclusions could be drawn". — Source: Hardell et al. 1999, summarised in EEA 2013 ch. 21 §21.3; Date: 1999

Q7. [paraphrase] Two Swedish epidemiologists wrote to the journal suggesting that selection bias might explain the high response rate in the Swedish study. The authors rebutted the point. — Source: Ahlbom and Feychting 1999; Hardell et al. 1999b; as summarised in EEA 2013 ch. 21 §21.6; Date: 1999

Q8. [paraphrase] A US hospital-based case-control study found no detectable increase in brain tumour risk, and a US case-control study published in *JAMA* in 2000 also found no association. Among 782 brain tumour patients in the hospital-based study, only 22 had used a mobile phone for 5 years or more. — Source: Inskip et al. 2001 *NEJM*; Muscat et al. 2000 *JAMA*; EEA 2013 ch. 21 §21.3; Date: 2000–2001

Q9. An accompanying editorial stated that "the use of cellular telephones does not detectably increase the risk of brain tumors" and that "This study allays fears raised by alarmist reports". — Source: Trichopoulos and Adami 2001, as quoted in EEA 2013 ch. 21 §21.3; Date: 2001

Q10. [paraphrase] A Danish study of mobile subscribers (about 420 000 subscriptions, 1982–1995) found no raised cancer incidence. Exposure was defined by subscription, not by measured use. — Source: Johansen et al. 2001, *J Natl Cancer Inst* 93:203–207; EEA 2013 ch. 21 (Danish cohort discussion); Date: 2001

Q11. [paraphrase] Before the international case-control study (the Interphone study) began, one of the Swedish scientists in it co-signed an opinion letter calling a link between mobile phones and brain tumours "biologically bizarre". — Source: Adami et al. 2001, as summarised in EEA 2013 ch. 21 §21.6; Date: 2001

Q12. [paraphrase] The Interphone study, an international case-control study of brain tumours and mobile phone use coordinated by the WHO International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), began in 2000 in 13 countries. Industry contributed about EUR 5.5 million of its funding. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 21 §21.9 (citing IARC 2010); Date: 2000–2004

Q13. [paraphrase] The Swedish group's later studies (cases diagnosed 1997–2003) cost about EUR 410 000. They were funded by the Swedish Work Environment Fund, cancer charities, a hospital cancer fund and the telecom operator Telia. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 21 footnote 11; Date: 1997–2004

Q14. [paraphrase] The Swedish second study (cases 1997–2000), which also asked about cordless phones, reported raised risks for analogue phone use, especially for tumours on the side of the head where the phone was held. — Source: Hardell et al. 2002, as summarised in EEA 2013 ch. 21 §21.4; Date: 2002

Q15. [paraphrase] A review commissioned by the Swedish Radiation Protection Authority suggested that leaving out deceased cases was a source of bias in the Swedish studies. — Source: Boice and McLaughlin 2002, cited in EEA 2013 ch. 21 §21.5; Date: 2002

Q16. [paraphrase] IARC classified extremely-low-frequency magnetic fields (as from power lines) as Group 2B, "possibly carcinogenic to humans". — Source: IARC Monograph vol. 80, cited in EEA 2013 ch. 21 §21.1; Date: 2002

Q17. [paraphrase] It took almost 50 years after the atomic bombings to establish that they had also caused brain tumours (meningiomas). Brain tumour latency can run to decades. — Source: Shibata et al., *Lancet* 344:1770, cited in EEA 2013 ch. 21 Box 21.1 note 6; Date: 1994

Q18. [paraphrase] Modelling suggested that radiofrequency energy penetrates deeper, relative to head size, in children's heads than in adults'. — Source: Gandhi, Lazzi and Furse 1996, *IEEE Trans Microwave Theory Tech* 44:1884–1897; Date: 1996

Q19. [paraphrase] A UK independent expert group found the balance of evidence did not show health harm at exposures below guidelines. It recommended a precautionary approach, including discouraging non-essential use by children, and more research. — Source: Independent Expert Group on Mobile Phones (Stewart Report), *Mobile Phones and Health* (2000); Date: 2000

Q20. [paraphrase] International exposure guidelines (ICNIRP 1998) and the US limit on specific absorption rate (1.6 W/kg, 1996) were set to prevent tissue heating. They did not address non-thermal or long-term effects such as cancer. — Source: ICNIRP, *Health Physics* 74:494–522 (1998); US FCC rules (1996); Date: 1996–1998

Q21. [paraphrase] An EU-funded laboratory project of 12 groups in 7 countries reported that radiofrequency fields could cause DNA strand breaks in some cultured cells, while calling the findings preliminary. — Source: REFLEX final report to the European Commission, Dec 2004; Date: 2004

Q22. [paraphrase] Mobile phone subscriptions worldwide passed 1 billion, and mobile overtook fixed lines in many countries. Benefits went to almost all users, while revenues were concentrated among a few handset makers and network operators. — Source: ITU *World Telecommunication Development Report* 2002/2003; Date: 2002

Q23. [paraphrase] European governments raised very large sums auctioning third-generation mobile licences: about GBP 22.5 billion in the UK and about EUR 50 billion in Germany. States therefore had a direct fiscal stake in the network rollout. — Source: UK Radiocommunications Agency auction results (April 2000); German RegTP auction (August 2000); Date: 2000
