# Evidence packet K07

- **Label:** Second-hand (environmental) tobacco smoke breathed by non-smokers.
- **De-identification:** Not feasible; the product is unmistakable. Names are kept. Coders may know the outcome; score only from the items below. The EEA chapter (2013, ch. 7) covers second-hand smoke, not active smoking, so that is the hazard in this case. Active-smoking items appear only as prior history (M).
- **t0:** 1981. This is the first credible early warning as given by EEA (2013, ch. 7, §7.2: "Seminal epidemiological studies in 1981 demonstrated that second-hand smoke exposure was associated with lung cancer (Hirayama, 1981)"). EEA Table 7.2 places the first studies in the "1970–1980s".
- **Scoring point:** t0 + 5 = 1986. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

## Evidence

Q1. [paraphrase] In a large Japanese prospective cohort of non-smoking wives, lung-cancer mortality was higher in wives of smokers than in wives of non-smokers. The risk rose with the husband's consumption, to roughly twice the baseline for wives of heavy smokers. — Source: Hirayama 1981, *BMJ* 282:183–185; summarised in EEA 2013, ch. 7, §7.2; Date: 1981.

Q2. [paraphrase] A case–control study in Athens reported a similar association between husbands' smoking and lung cancer in non-smoking wives. — Source: Trichopoulos et al. 1981, *Int J Cancer* 27:1–4; Date: 1981.

Q3. [paraphrase] An analysis of the American Cancer Society cohort found only a small, statistically non-significant excess in lung cancer among non-smoking wives of smokers. — Source: Garfinkel 1981, *J Natl Cancer Inst* 66:1061–1066; Date: 1981.

Q4. [paraphrase] Second-hand smoke is a complex mixture of thousands of gases and fine particles from burning tobacco and exhaled smoke. During the 1970s and 1980s, data on its harmful effects began to appear in the scientific literature. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 7, §7.2; Date: 1970s–1981.

Q5. [paraphrase] The exposed population is the partners and children of smokers, and bartenders and other workers in smoky environments. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 7, chapter summary; Date: (population description; applies throughout).

Q6. [paraphrase] Infants whose parents smoked had more pneumonia and bronchitis in their first year. — Source: Colley, Holland and Corkhill 1974, *Lancet* 2:1031–1034; Date: 1974.

Q7. [paraphrase] The US Surgeon General's 1972 report on smoking included a section on public exposure to air pollution from tobacco smoke. — Source: US DHEW 1972, *The Health Consequences of Smoking*; Date: 1972.

Q8. [paraphrase] Evidence of harm from second-hand smoke "was clearly a threat to the tobacco industry as early as the 1970s". Restrictions on smoking could reduce daily cigarette consumption and sales. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 7, §7.2; Date: 1970s.

Q9. "The strategic and long-run antidote to the passive smoking issue is, as we see it, developing and widely publicizing clear-cut, credible, medical evidence that passive smoking is not harmful to the non-smoker's health." This is from a report prepared for the Tobacco Institute, internal and not public at the time. — Source: Roper Organization 1978, as quoted in EEA 2013, ch. 7, §7.3; Date: 1978.

Q10. [paraphrase] The industry's strategies included funding and publishing research that supports its position, hiding its involvement, suppressing and criticising unfavourable research, changing scientific standards, and taking its interpretation to the lay press and directly to policymakers. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 7, §7.1 and Box 7.1; Date: practices described as constant from the 1950s onward.

Q11. [paraphrase] Industry lawyers and executives, rather than scientists, controlled the design, conduct and dissemination of industry-sponsored research. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 7, §7.1; Date: 1970s–1980s (as applied to second-hand smoke).

Q12. [paraphrase] After the 1981 Japanese study (Q1), the industry publicly attacked its statistics. Internal documents show that consultants it engaged privately concluded the criticism was not valid. — Source: Hong and Bero 2002, *BMJ* 325:1413 (analysis of internal documents dated 1981); Date: 1981.

Q13. [paraphrase] Prior history: in 1953 a UK Government advisory committee concluded that the association between smoking and lung cancer was causal. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 7, Table 7.2; Date: 1953.

Q14. [paraphrase] Prior history: in 1954, publication of studies linking tobacco to cancer, with media coverage and falling sales, was called the "1954 emergency" inside the industry. The industry responded with a public-relations campaign (Hill and Knowlton) to "manufacture doubt" about the link without denying it outright. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 7, Table 7.2; Date: 1954.

Q15. [paraphrase] Prior history: the 1964 US Surgeon General's report concluded that there is a causal relationship between excessive smoking and lung cancer. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 7, Table 7.2; Date: 1964.

Q16. [paraphrase] The US cigarette market was dominated by six manufacturers (Philip Morris, R.J. Reynolds, Brown & Williamson, American Brands, Lorillard and Liggett). They were represented jointly by the Tobacco Institute (founded 1958) and funded research through the Council for Tobacco Research (founded 1954 as the Tobacco Industry Research Committee). — Source: industry structure as documented in Bero 2003/2005 and EEA 2013, ch. 7; Date: 1954–1986.

Q17. [paraphrase] Non-smokers' rights groups had formed before t0, for example Action on Smoking and Health (US, 1967) and the Group Against Smoking Pollution (GASP, 1971). — Source: organisational founding records; Date: 1967–1971.

Q18. [paraphrase] A New Jersey court ordered an employer to restrict smoking in an employee's work area, holding that the employer's duty to provide a safe workplace covered tobacco smoke. — Source: *Shimp v. New Jersey Bell Telephone Co.*, 368 A.2d 408 (N.J. Super. Ch. 1976); Date: 1976.

Q19. [paraphrase] A 1978 California ballot initiative to restrict smoking in public places was defeated after heavy spending by tobacco companies against it. — Source: California Secretary of State, 1978 Proposition 5 results; contemporary press; Date: 1978 [abstain: unverifiable — spending figure not confirmed, so none is given].

Q20. [paraphrase] The harm has a long latency: lung cancer appears decades after exposure. Relative risks in the 1981 studies were modest (about 1.2–2), so the harm is detectable only statistically and is not distinguishable in an individual case. — Source: Hirayama 1981; Garfinkel 1981; Date: 1981.

Q21. [paraphrase] A National Research Council committee estimated that non-smoking spouses of smokers have about a 30% higher lung-cancer risk than non-smoking spouses of non-smokers. It noted that exposure is hard to measure. — Source: National Research Council 1986, *Environmental Tobacco Smoke: Measuring Exposures and Assessing Health Effects*; EEA 2013, ch. 7, §7.2; Date: 1986.

Q22. [paraphrase] The US Surgeon General concluded that involuntary smoking causes disease, including lung cancer, in healthy non-smokers. It found that children of smokers have more respiratory infections and symptoms, and that separating smokers and non-smokers within the same airspace may reduce exposure but does not eliminate it. — Source: US DHHS 1986, *The Health Consequences of Involuntary Smoking*; EEA 2013, ch. 7, §7.2; Date: 1986.

Q23. [paraphrase] The early independent research came from academic and government epidemiologists (Japan, Greece, the UK, the American Cancer Society). In parallel, industry funded and disseminated its own research to counter it. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 7, §§7.2–7.3; Date: 1974–1986.
