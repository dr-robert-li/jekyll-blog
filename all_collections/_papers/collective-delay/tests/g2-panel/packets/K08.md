# Evidence packet K08

- **Label:** Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) used as aerosol propellants, refrigerants and foam-blowing agents, and their effect on stratospheric ozone.
- **De-identification:** Not feasible; the chemistry is the evidence. Names are kept. Coders may know the outcome; score only from the items below.
- **t0:** 1974. This is the first credible early warning as given by EEA (2001, ch. 7, Table 7.1 and §7.4: Molina and Rowland's 1974 theoretical argument that CFCs would destroy stratospheric ozone).
- **Scoring point:** t0 + 5 = 1979. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

## Evidence

Q1. [paraphrase] Laboratory work had shown that chlorine photosensitises the decomposition of ozone. — Source: Weigert 1907; Norrish and Neville 1934; EEA 2001, ch. 7, Table 7.1; Date: 1907, 1934.

Q2. [paraphrase] In 1929 Thomas Midgley at General Motors recommended CFC-12 and CFC-11 as refrigerants because they were efficient, non-toxic and non-flammable. Du Pont began industrial production of CFC-12 in 1930 and CFC-11 in 1934. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 7, §7.3; Date: 1929–1934.

Q3. [paraphrase] Cumulative releases of CFC-12 and CFC-11 were only 25 and 5 kilotonnes over 1930–1948. By 1970 annual releases had reached 300 and 207 kilotonnes. "People had been persuaded to buy CFCs and throw them away (aerosols)." — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 7, §7.6; Date: 1930–1970.

Q4. [paraphrase] Human effects on the ozone layer became an international issue in 1970, first over nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide and water from planned fleets of supersonic stratospheric transport (SST) aircraft. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 7, §7.4; Date: 1970–1971.

Q5. [paraphrase] The SST concern was acted on: the United States abandoned development of SSTs. This gave an earlier precedent of stratospheric-ozone concern influencing a technology decision. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 7, §7.4; Date: 1971.

Q6. [paraphrase] A survey with a newly developed device able to measure trace CFCs showed that CFCs, released mostly in the northern hemisphere, had spread worldwide. Their concentrations implied that removal from the lower atmosphere must be extremely slow, so virtually all the CFCs released were still in the atmosphere. — Source: Lovelock, Maggs and Wade 1973, *Nature* 241:194; EEA 2001, ch. 7, §7.4; Date: 1973.

Q7. [paraphrase] CFCs are so stable that they would eventually reach the stratosphere. There they would release chlorine by photolysis, and an ozone-destroying chain reaction would follow. — Source: Molina and Rowland 1974, *Nature* 249:810; Cicerone, Stolarski and Walters 1974, *Science* 185:1165; EEA 2001, ch. 7, §7.4; Date: 1974.

Q8. [paraphrase] The proposed harm is invisible and delayed. CFCs are long-lived (CFC-12's lifetime is of the order of 100 years), so ozone loss would build up over decades after release. The health effect expected from more ultraviolet radiation, chiefly skin cancer, has an average latency of 30–40 years. It adds to a background rate of skin cancer that was already rising. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 7, §7.1 and Figure 7.1 caption; Date: mechanism as argued 1974–1976.

Q9. [paraphrase] At the time there was no observed ozone loss attributable to CFCs. The 1974 warning was a theoretical and model-based argument. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 7, §7.4 (describes the 1974 papers as theoretical arguments); Date: 1974.

Q10. "A vigorous debate followed." This refers to the 1974 papers. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 7, §7.4; Date: 1974–1976.

Q11. "Should reputable evidence show that some fluorocarbons cause a health hazard through depletion of the ozone layer, we are prepared to stop production of the offending compounds." This was Du Pont, "the world's first and largest producer of CFCs", in a full-page newspaper advertisement. — Source: *New York Times*, 30 June 1975, as quoted in EEA 2001, ch. 7, §7.4; Date: 1975.

Q12. [paraphrase] The industry, through the (then) Manufacturing Chemists Association, gave substantial financial support to institutes and individuals researching the ozone problem. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 7, §7.4; Date: from 1972 onward.

Q13. [paraphrase] The United States produced about 46% of the world's CFC-11 and CFC-12 in 1974. Production was concentrated in a small number of chemical companies. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 7, §7.4 (46% figure for 1974); Date: 1974.

Q14. [paraphrase] The CFC industry "had expanded enormously throughout the 1960s". Uses included aerosol propellants, refrigeration and air conditioning, and foam blowing. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 7, §§7.3–7.4; Date: 1960s–1974.

Q15. [paraphrase] In the United States an effective public campaign, stimulated by the earlier supersonic-aircraft debate, built up around CFCs in aerosols. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 7, §7.4; Date: 1974–1976.

Q16. [paraphrase] A major US household-products maker, S.C. Johnson, announced in 1975 that it would stop using CFC propellants in its aerosol products. — Source: contemporary press reports, June 1975; Date: 1975 [abstain: non-authoritative source — company announcement reached only through secondary accounts].

Q17. [paraphrase] The US National Academy of Sciences concluded that continued CFC releases would reduce stratospheric ozone. It gave a central estimate of eventual depletion of several per cent at then-current release rates, with a wide uncertainty range. — Source: National Research Council 1976, *Halocarbons: Effects on Stratospheric Ozone*; Date: 1976.

Q18. [paraphrase] The SST debate had already linked stratospheric ozone loss to skin cancer through more UV-B. This prior framing was available to policymakers when the CFC warning arrived. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 7, §7.4 (public campaign "stimulated by the earlier debate on the impact of supersonic aircraft"); Date: 1971–1975.

Q19. [paraphrase] The early warning came from independent academic scientists (university and government laboratory researchers). Industry-funded research ran in parallel (Q12). — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 7, §7.4; Date: 1973–1976.

Q20. [paraphrase] The harm is global and spatially diffuse. CFCs released in one hemisphere spread worldwide (Q6), so those who bear the harm are not limited to the countries that produce or use CFCs. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 7, §7.4; Date: 1973.

Q21. [paraphrase] Scientific measurement of atmospheric ozone had a long tradition, from Fabry and Buisson's pioneering measurement to the work of Dobson and colleagues at Oxford. An observing infrastructure therefore existed before the warning. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 7, §7.2; Date: 1910s–1950s.
