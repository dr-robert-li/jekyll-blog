# Evidence packet K13

- **Label:** Solvent S, a volatile aromatic hydrocarbon distilled from coal tar, used to dissolve rubber in rubber-goods manufacture and in dry cleaning.
- **De-identification:** Partial. The substance is not named, but its source and uses are given because the evidence depends on them, so coders may be able to identify it and may know the outcome. Score only from the items below.
- **t0:** 1897. EEA gives the 1897 Swedish report of fatal blood-marrow failure in rubber-goods workers, and a French report the same year, as the first early warning.
- **Scoring point:** t0 + 5 = 1902. Every item below is dated at or before this year.
- **Note on evidence density:** The EEA chapter has little on 1897–1902, so several items are general context from that period (other hazards, institutions) rather than items about Solvent S.

## Evidence

Q1. [paraphrase] A Swedish pharmacology professor reported chronic poisoning, with four deaths, among young women working with a rubber solution made with Solvent S. The women had aplastic anaemia (bone-marrow failure). — Source: Santesson, C.G., "Über chronische Vergiftungen mit Steinkohlenteerbenzin: vier Todesfälle", *Archiv für Hygiene* 31:336–376, as summarised in EEA 2001 *Late lessons I* ch. 4 §4.1; Date: 1897

Q2. [paraphrase] Sources disagree on the workplace. EEA says women "manufacturing raincoats" in one passage and "bicycle tyres" in another; later reviews say a bicycle-tyre factory. One review says nine women were affected and four died within 1–4 months of starting exposure. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 4 §4.1 and §4.3.1; Aksoy 1985 *Am J Ind Med* 8:9–20; *Eur J Oncol Environ Health* timeline (Mattioli 1885); Date: 1897

Q3. [paraphrase] The same year, two French physicians reported haemorrhaging in a young man working in a dry-cleaning operation exposed to the solvent. — Source: LeNoir and Claude 1897, as summarised in EEA 2001 ch. 4 §4.1; Date: 1897

Q4. [paraphrase] The harm was serious and fast: affected workers showed weakness, abnormal bleeding and anaemia, and deaths came within months of first exposure. — Source: Santesson 1897, as summarised in the *Eur J Oncol Environ Health* timeline and EEA 2001 ch. 4; Date: 1897

Q5. [paraphrase] The Swedish author called the solvent "coal-tar benzine" (Steinkohlenteerbenzin). At the time, coal-tar distillates and petroleum spirits were sold under similar names, which made it hard to tell which product a worker had been exposed to. — Source: Santesson 1897 (title as cited); EEA 2001 ch. 4 references; Date: 1897

Q6. [paraphrase] Acute poisoning by the solvent's vapour was already on record. *The Lancet* reported a "new domestic poison" with a death from inhaling the vapour (1862). A London hospital report described acute poisoning by "benzoline" (1878), and a fatal accidental overdose was reported in 1889. — Source: *Eur J Oncol Environ Health* historical timeline (citing *Lancet* 1862; St George's Hospital Reports 1878; Averill 1889); Date: 1862–1889

Q7. [paraphrase] Industrial use of the solvent had begun in the second half of the 19th century. Its chronic toxicity was first seriously considered in 1897. — Source: Aksoy 1985, *Am J Ind Med* 8:9–20; Date: 1897

Q8. [paraphrase] The solvent was valued as an excellent rubber solvent and was cheaper than alternatives. It was obtained as a by-product of coal-tar distillation, linked to the gas and coke industries. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 4 §4.3.1 ("such a good rubber solvent"; cheaper than other solvents); Date: 19th century context

Q9. [paraphrase] Those harmed were mostly young female factory workers in small rubber-goods workshops, plus individual workers in dry-cleaning shops. The general public was not identified as exposed. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 4 §4.1; Santesson 1897; Date: 1897

Q10. [paraphrase] The 1897 reports were case reports by individual academic physicians and a university pharmacologist. They were not commissioned by the solvent's producers or users. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 4 §4.1 and §4.3.1; Date: 1897

Q11. [paraphrase] Other occupational poisons were already recognised and sometimes regulated: lead in the potteries (UK Potteries Regulations 1867), phosphorus necrosis ("phossy jaw") in match-making, mercury and arsenic. — Source: EEA 2013 *Late lessons II* ch. 3 §3.2 (lead, potteries 1867); standard occupational-health histories; Date: before 1897

Q12. [paraphrase] The UK Factory and Workshop Act 1895 made poisoning by lead, phosphorus and arsenic, and anthrax, notifiable to the factory inspectorate. Poisoning by organic solvents was not on the list. — Source: Factory and Workshop Act 1895 (UK); Date: 1895

Q13. [paraphrase] A UK woman factory inspector reported harm from asbestos dust in 1898, which shows that inspectorates were beginning to record dust and chemical hazards in textile and manufacturing trades. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 5 (Deane 1898); Date: 1898

Q14. [paraphrase] Sweden's 1889 worker-protection law (lagen om skydd mot yrkesfara) set up a state factory inspectorate with powers over dangerous trades. — Source: Swedish Act on Protection against Occupational Hazards 1889; Date: 1889

Q15. [paraphrase] Compensation schemes then covered industrial accidents, not occupational diseases: the German accident insurance law of 1884 and the UK Workmen's Compensation Act 1897. Employers therefore carried little direct cost when workers fell ill with a chemical disease. — Source: German Unfallversicherungsgesetz 1884; UK Workmen's Compensation Act 1897; Date: 1884–1897

Q16. [paraphrase] Linking the disease to the solvent needed a specialist investigation. Aplastic anaemia had other causes, and only a pharmacologist examining the production process identified the rubber solvent as the probable cause. — Source: McGill Office for Science and Society, "A tale of benzene poisoning"; Santesson 1897; Date: 1897

Q17. [paraphrase] Early warnings were published in German- and French-language medical and hygiene journals, which were read mainly by physicians and hygienists, not by factory owners or labour organisations. — Source: Santesson 1897 (*Archiv für Hygiene*); LeNoir and Claude 1897 (French clinical literature) as cited in EEA 2001 ch. 4; Date: 1897

Q18. [paraphrase] The solvent was one of many inputs to a growing rubber-goods trade (bicycle tyres, waterproof garments) during the 1890s cycling boom. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 4 §4.1 and §4.3.1 (raincoat and tyre manufacture); Aksoy 1985; Date: 1890s
