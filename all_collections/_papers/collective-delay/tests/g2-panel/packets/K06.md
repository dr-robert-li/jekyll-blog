# Evidence packet K06

- **Label:** Antimicrobial drugs added to livestock feed as growth promoters.
- **De-identification:** Not feasible without losing meaning (the agent class and setting are what the evidence is about). Names are kept. Coders may know the outcome; score only from the items below.
- **t0:** 1969. This is the first early warning as given by EEA (2001, ch. 9, section 9.2 "The first early warning": the UK Swann Committee report).
- **Scoring point:** t0 + 5 = 1974. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

## Evidence

Q1. [paraphrase] Researchers found the growth-promoting effect in the late 1940s. Chickens fed fermentation waste from tetracycline production grew faster than the controls, and the effect came from residual tetracycline, not from the vitamin B12 in the waste. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 9, §9.1.1, citing Stokstad and Jukes 1949; Date: 1949.

Q2. [paraphrase] Stokstad and Jukes, the researchers who reported the effect, worked for Lederle Laboratories, the pharmaceutical division of American Cyanamid, which made tetracycline. — Source: Stokstad and Jukes 1949, *Proc. Soc. Exp. Biol. Med.* 73:523 (author affiliation); Date: 1949.

Q3. [paraphrase] Long-term feeding of sub-therapeutic antimicrobial doses was "readily adopted" and became an integral part of the production systems of industrialised animal husbandry. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 9, §9.1.1; Date: 1950s–1960s.

Q4. [paraphrase] Besides faster growth and better feed conversion, low doses were reported to raise egg production in laying hens, litter size in sows and milk yield in dairy cows. They also allowed early weaning and high stocking densities. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 9, §9.1.1; Date: 1950s–1960s.

Q5. [paraphrase] Growth-promoter antimicrobials were sold over the counter, while antimicrobials for therapy were mostly prescription-only. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 9, §9.1.1; Date: 1950s–1960s.

Q6. [paraphrase] In 1953 the UK legalised adding antibiotics to animal feed for growth promotion, without a veterinary prescription, despite warnings from Ministry of Health officials. — Source: Kirchhelle 2020, *Pyrrhic Progress*, ch. "Swann Song" (NCBI Bookshelf NBK554185); Date: 1953.

Q7. [paraphrase] The earlier UK Penicillin Act 1947 had made antibiotics prescription-only, partly because of fears about resistance. — Source: Kirchhelle 2020 (NCBI Bookshelf NBK554185); Date: 1947.

Q8. "Microbes are educated to resist penicillin." This is the discoverer of penicillin, Alexander Fleming, warning about misuse. — Source: *New York Times*, 26 June 1945, p. 21, as quoted in EEA 2001, ch. 9, §9.1.2; Date: 1945.

Q9. [paraphrase] By the early 1950s the medical, veterinary and pharmaceutical press widely acknowledged antimicrobial resistance as a problem. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 9, §9.1.2; Date: early 1950s.

Q10. [paraphrase] In the 1960s it was shown that bacteria can acquire resistance by taking up existing genes on mobile genetic elements. Such resistance can spread to other clones, other species and even other genera (horizontal transmission), not only by mutation within one clone. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 9, §9.1.2; Date: 1960s.

Q11. [paraphrase] In the mid-1960s, food-borne infections in the UK with multi-drug-resistant salmonella caused growing concern. Calf-rearing was implicated. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 9, §9.2.1; Kirchhelle 2020; Date: mid-1960s.

Q12. [paraphrase] In 1968 the UK government set up an independent advisory committee, chaired by Professor Michael Swann, to examine transferable resistance and its consequences for human and animal health from growth-promoter and veterinary use. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 9, §9.2.1; Date: 1968.

Q13. The Swann Committee judged the available data "a sufficiently sound basis for action". — Source: Swann 1969, as quoted in EEA 2001, ch. 9, §9.2.1; Date: 1969.

Q14. [paraphrase] Swann proposed that antibiotics supplied without prescription for feed be limited to those that (a) are of economic value in UK livestock production, (b) have little or no application as therapeutic agents in humans or animals, and (c) will not impair the efficacy of prescribed therapeutic antibiotics through resistance. — Source: Swann 1969, as quoted in EEA 2001, ch. 9, §9.2.1; Date: 1969.

Q15. "Tylosin should not be available without prescription for use as a 'feed' antibiotic." — Source: Swann 1969, as quoted in EEA 2001, ch. 9, §9.2.1; Date: 1969.

Q16. [paraphrase] Swann also recommended a single permanent committee with overall responsibility for all uses of antibiotics: in humans, animals, food preservation and other purposes. — Source: Swann 1969, as quoted in EEA 2001, ch. 9, §9.2.1; Date: 1969.

Q17. [paraphrase] The Swann recommendations rested on "less than full scientific certainty". They prompted much debate and calls for more research, and met strong opposition from the pharmaceutical industry and the farming community in the UK. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 9, §9.3.1; Date: 1969–1970.

Q18. [paraphrase] Feed-antibiotic manufacturers lobbied the agriculture ministry directly. For example, Cyanamid of Great Britain wrote to the minister in August 1970. — Source: Kirchhelle 2020 (NCBI Bookshelf NBK554185); Date: 1970.

Q19. [paraphrase] Competing government departments (agriculture and health) were involved, with different priorities for livestock productivity and for medical efficacy. — Source: Kirchhelle 2020 (NCBI Bookshelf NBK554185); Date: 1969–1971.

Q20. [paraphrase] The harm is indirect. Resistance selected in animal bacteria may reach humans through the food chain or through gene transfer, and it appears as treatment failure in human or animal infections, not as a distinctive disease. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 9, §§9.1.2, 9.2.1 (mechanism as understood in the 1960s); Date: 1960s.


Q21. [paraphrase] The early warning came from a government-appointed independent committee, drawing on public-health laboratory work on transferable resistance. The early evidence of benefit (growth promotion) came from industry laboratories (see Q1–Q2). — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 9, §9.2.1; Kirchhelle 2020; Date: 1965–1969.
