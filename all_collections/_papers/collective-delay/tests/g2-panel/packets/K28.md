# Evidence packet K28
Label: Smoke and sulphur dioxide from coal burning in a large city (London, December 1952 smog)
De-identification: not feasible: names kept; coders may know the outcome
t0 (first credible early warning): 1952
Scoring point: t0 + 5 = 1957. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

Note on t0: the EEA case chapter covers two hazards from sulphur dioxide: urban smog, which harms human lungs, and long-range acidification of lakes and forests, warned of in 1968. Its table opens with the December 1952 smog, so t0 = 1952 and this packet covers the urban-smog hazard. The outcomes file records the 1968 alternative.

Sources: "EEA ch. 10" = EEA (2001) *Late lessons from early warnings* I, ch. 10 (Semb). "GLA 2002" = Greater London Authority, *50 years on: the struggle for air quality in London since the great smog of December 1952*, which summarises the 1953 London County Council measurements, the 1953 interim report of the government Committee on Air Pollution and the 1954 Ministry of Health report (*Mortality and morbidity during the London fog of December 1952*, Reports on Public Health and Medical Subjects No. 95). "Met Office" = Met Office case study "The Great Smog of 1952". Only material describing events up to 1957 is used.

## Evidence
Q1. [paraphrase] A dense fog mixed with coal smoke covered London from Friday 5 to Tuesday 9 December 1952. At night visibility fell to a few metres, and pedestrians could not find their way even in familiar districts. — Source: Met Office; Date: 1952
Q2. [paraphrase] After a very cold November and early December, Londoners were burning large quantities of coal at home. An anticyclone created a temperature inversion that trapped smoke from house chimneys and factories, along with pollution carried by east winds from the continent. — Source: Met Office; Date: 1952
Q3. [paraphrase] At London County Council headquarters, smoke rose from 0.49 to 4.46 mg/m³ and sulphur dioxide from 0.41 to 3.83 mg/m³, the highest readings since measurements began there in 1932. — Source: GLA 2002, citing London County Council 1953; Date: 1952–1953
Q4. [paraphrase] Measurements by scientists at St Bartholomew's Hospital showed concentrations of smoke particles and sulphur dioxide of several milligrams per cubic metre. — Source: EEA ch. 10; Date: 1952
Q5. [paraphrase] The first reported casualties were prize cattle at the Smithfield Show. The human death toll became apparent only afterwards, first to coroners, pathologists and registrars of deaths. — Source: GLA 2002; Date: December 1952
Q6. [paraphrase] By comparing registered deaths with those expected, the Ministry of Health committee estimated that 3 500–4 000 more people died than normal, with a figure nearer 4 000 the best estimate. The chief medical statistician compared the toll to the cholera epidemic of 1854 and the influenza epidemic of 1918. — Source: GLA 2002, citing Ministry of Health 1954; Date: 1954
Q7. [paraphrase] Respiratory diseases accounted for 59% and 76% of the excess deaths in the two weeks after the fog, with bronchitis and emphysema most prominent; cardiovascular disease accounted for 22% and 16%. The greatest effect was on people over 65 and those already chronically ill. — Source: GLA 2002, citing Ministry of Health 1954; Date: 1954
Q8. [paraphrase] As far as could be ascertained, no previously healthy person died; for most healthy people the smog was little more than a nuisance. — Source: GLA 2002, citing Ministry of Health 1954; Date: 1954
Q9. [paraphrase] Sickness benefit claims rose 108% in the week ending 16 December compared with the average of the three previous years. — Source: GLA 2002, citing Ministry of Pensions and National Insurance statistics; Date: 1952
Q10. [paraphrase] The committee concluded that the main irritants were probably derived from burning coal and coal products, but that with the knowledge then available it was impossible to say any one pollutant caused the deaths. — Source: GLA 2002, citing Ministry of Health 1954; Date: 1954
Q11. [paraphrase] The government's interim air-pollution inquiry named tarry particles and sulphur oxides as the most likely causes of ill health. An industrial exposure limit for sulphur dioxide of 2.86 mg/m³ had been recommended, but there was evidence that much lower concentrations had adverse effects. — Source: GLA 2002, citing the Committee on Air Pollution interim report (December 1953); Date: 1953
Q12. "The domestic fire is the biggest single smoke producer." — Source: Committee on Air Pollution interim report, quoted in GLA 2002. The report adds that, per unit of coal burnt, the domestic fire produces twice as much smoke as industry and discharges it at a lower level. Date: 1953
Q13. [paraphrase] London was entirely dependent on coal for heating and energy. Coal contains sulphur, which can only partly be removed before use. — Source: EEA ch. 10; Date: 1952
Q14. [paraphrase] Oil from the Middle East became available in large quantities after 1950. Much of the refined output was heavy fuel oil with 2.5–3% sulphur, used in new power stations, large boilers and industry. — Source: EEA ch. 10; Date: 1950s
Q15. [paraphrase] The London County Council produced a report in January 1953 detailing the smog's effects. — Source: GLA 2002; Date: 1953
Q16. "I am not satisfied that further general legislation is needed at present." — Source: the Minister for Housing and Local Government, quoted in GLA 2002, which says the government's first response was to deny responsibility and urge local authorities to use powers they already had; Date: 1953
Q17. "anyone would think fog had only started in London since I became a Minister" — Source: the Minister of Health, as reported in the *Evening Standard*, quoted in GLA 2002; Date: 1953
Q18. [paraphrase] Doctors suggested that smog masks be worn, and office workers tried out the new masks. — Source: GLA 2002 (photograph caption, October 1953); Date: 1953
Q19. [paraphrase] Severe smogs had occurred before. A December 1873 fog raised London's death rate 40% above normal, and marked increases followed fogs in 1880, 1882, 1891, 1892 and November 1948. — Source: Met Office; Date: 1873–1948
Q20. [paraphrase] Worse smog incidents had occurred previously, and air quality in London and other UK cities was improving, not deteriorating. What was new was the post-war political and social set-up: the public was no longer willing to accept the situation. — Source: EEA ch. 10; Date: 1952
Q21. [paraphrase] A thirteenth-century royal proclamation prohibited burning sea coal in London. In 1661 John Evelyn's *Fumifugium* argued that smoke shortened Londoners' lives, and in 1819 a parliamentary select committee concluded that smoke was prejudicial to health. — Source: GLA 2002; Date: 13th century–1819
Q22. [paraphrase] The Smoke Nuisance Abatement (Metropolis) Acts of 1853 and 1856 empowered London's police to act against furnaces, factories and steam vessels; the 1891 Public Health (London) Act moved responsibility to sanitary authorities. Smoke from coal burning in private houses remained a problem into the twentieth century. — Source: GLA 2002; Date: 1853–early 1900s
Q23. [paraphrase] Prosecutions required proof that the smoke was "black", so convictions were rare. Industrialists charged with smoke pollution commonly pointed out that 95% of the smoke came from the chimneys of London's 700 000 houses. — Source: GLA 2002; Date: early 20th century
Q24. [paraphrase] The London County Council once took the view that the open fire was so essential a feature of national home life that any attempt to abolish it was almost out of the question. — Source: GLA 2002; Date: early 20th century
