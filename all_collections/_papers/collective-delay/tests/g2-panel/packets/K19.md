# Evidence packet K19

**Label:** A synthetic oestrogen (diethylstilboestrol) prescribed to patients.
**De-identification:** Not feasible. The compound is identified by its synthesis history even when unnamed, so coders may know the outcome. Score only from the items below.
**t0:** 1938, the first report of increased cancer incidence in animals given the compound.
**Scoring point:** t0 + 5 = 1943. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

## Evidence

Q1. [paraphrase] Charles Dodds and colleagues report the synthesis of diethylstilboestrol, the first orally active synthetic oestrogen. — Source: Dodds et al., Nature (5 Feb 1938), as described in EEA 2001, ch. 8 §8.2; Date: 1938

Q2. [paraphrase] The work comes from British academic laboratories: the Courtauld Institute (Middlesex Hospital) and Robinson's laboratory at Oxford. Dodds is not connected with any drug manufacturer and works under a Medical Research Council grant. — Source: DES history sites summarising the 1938 publication (diethylstilbestrol.co.uk; desaction.org); Date: 1938

Q3. [paraphrase] The compound is not patented, in line with a Medical Research Council policy against patenting drugs discovered with public money. Many firms can therefore manufacture it. — Source: diethylstilbestrol.co.uk, "DES and the identification problem" (secondary); Date: 1938

Q4. [paraphrase] DES is "inexpensive and simple to synthesise". The developing pharmaceutical industry "quickly began worldwide production". — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 8 §8.2; Date: 1938–1939

Q5. [paraphrase] The compound is cheap to make from coal-tar derivatives, so competition keeps the price low from the start. — Source: diethylstilbestrol.co.uk (secondary); Date: 1938–1941

Q6. [paraphrase] Laboratory reports of increased cancer incidence in animals given oestrogens, including DES, begin to appear: mammary adenocarcinomas in male mice treated with a synthetic oestrogen. — Source: Lacassagne 1938, cited in EEA 2001, ch. 8 §8.3 and Table 8.1; Date: 1938

Q7. [paraphrase] Further animal reports follow, tying oestrogen administration to tumours. — Source: Geschickter 1939, cited in EEA 2001, ch. 8 §8.3; Date: 1939

Q8. [paraphrase] Mammary tumours are reported in mice given DES. — Source: Shimkin & Grady 1941; Greene & Brewer 1941, cited in EEA 2001, ch. 8 §8.3; Date: 1941

Q9. [paraphrase] The first report of DES given to patients describes its oestrogenic properties in humans. — Source: Bishop, Boycott & Zuckerman, Lancet 2:5–11 (1939), cited in EEA 2001, ch. 8 and Table 8.1; Date: 1939

Q10. [paraphrase] Like other drugs of the time, DES undergoes "very limited toxicological investigations". No long-term toxicity tests are conducted. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 8 §8.2; Date: 1938–1941

Q11. [paraphrase] DES quickly becomes popular in many treatments, including menopausal symptoms, prostate cancer and suppression of lactation. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 8 §8.2; Date: c. 1939–1943

Q12. [paraphrase] Because the drug is unpatented, many companies want to sell it in the US, and each must file a New Drug Application. At the regulator's request, they form a joint "small committee" in early 1941. They use a common pharmacopoeial standard, identical labelling, and a shared master file of clinical studies. — Source: diethylstilbestrol.co.uk (secondary account of FDA files); Date: 1941

Q13. [paraphrase] On 19 September 1941 the US regulator approves DES tablets (up to 5 mg) for gonorrhoeal vaginitis, atrophic vaginitis, menopausal symptoms and post-partum lactation suppression. Use in pregnancy is not among the approved uses. — Source: diethylstilbestrol.co.uk (secondary); Date: 1941

Q14. [paraphrase] The American Medical Association's Council on Pharmacy and Chemistry approves DES. — Source: EEA 2001, Table 8.1; Date: 1942

Q15. [paraphrase] The belief of the 1940s is that miscarriage results from a fall in oestrogen levels. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 8 §8.2 (citing Smith 1948 for the belief, which was current in the early 1940s); Date: early 1940s

Q16. [paraphrase] From 1941 a physician experiments with very high doses (100 mg intramuscularly into the cervix daily) in women "threatening to abort". He deems the drug effective and states there are no adverse consequences to mother or foetus. — Source: Karnaky 1942, as described in EEA 2001, ch. 8 §8.2; Date: 1942

Q17. [paraphrase] The first report of DES used to prevent miscarriage appears. — Source: EEA 2001, Table 8.1; Date: 1942

Q18. [paraphrase] Olive and George Smith (Boston) begin promoting DES in pregnancy through a series of uncontrolled trials. Their extensive publication on its effectiveness continues through the 1940s. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 8 §8.2; Date: early 1940s

Q19. [paraphrase] The prevailing medical view treats the foetal environment as a safe place, protected by the placental "barrier". — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 8 §8.1, describing the view held before the 1960s; Date: c. 1940
