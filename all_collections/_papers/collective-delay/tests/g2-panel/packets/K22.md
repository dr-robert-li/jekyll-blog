# Evidence packet K22

- **Label:** Overexploitation of a large demersal fish stock managed by a coastal state's fisheries department, with inshore and offshore fleets.
- **De-identification:** Partly feasible. The country, the stock and the reports are given neutral labels. Coders with fisheries knowledge may still recognise the case and know the outcome; score only from the items below.
- **Sub-case choice:** The EEA chapter (EEA 2001, ch. 2) covers several fisheries, and its chapter-level first warning (1376, Table A2.1) cannot be scored. The primary sub-case is the one with the longest treatment in the chapter (§2.4) and the one EEA's own summaries name. Its first dated early warning in the EEA table (Table 2.1 / A2.2) is used as t0.
- **t0:** 1986. EEA Table A2.2: a report commissioned by inshore fishers "indicates severe underestimation of the fishing pressure".
- **Scoring point:** t0 + 5 = 1991. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

## Evidence

Q1. [paraphrase] The stock, historically the largest of its species in the world, had been fished by European fleets since the 16th century. Fishing intensity rose sharply in the 1960s, to a peak catch of 800,000 tonnes in 1968, after which catches fell well below the levels the international regulatory body authorised. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4, citing DFO 2000; Date: 1960s–1970s.

Q2. [paraphrase] Many in the coastal state regarded the international commission responsible for the area, and its successor organisation, as ineffective. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4, citing O'Reilly Hinds 1995 and Day 1995; Date: 1970s–1980s.

Q3. [paraphrase] In the late 1970s the state used the new UN Law of the Sea to extend its jurisdiction from 12 to 200 nautical miles, partly to bring the stock under its own control. It set "deliberately conservative" catch limits aimed at taking about 20% of the stock, so that the stock could rebuild. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4; Date: 1977.

Q4. [paraphrase] By the 1970s managers relied on increasingly complex mathematical models and computing power to estimate stock size and fishing pressure, and there was optimism that past mistakes could be avoided. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4; Date: 1970s.

Q5. [paraphrase] The department's calculations indicated that the stock was rebuilding, and offshore catches by the state's own trawlers increased. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4; Date: 1977–1986.

Q6. [paraphrase] Inshore fishers had generally not upgraded their technology, unlike the offshore fleet. They could not reconcile their falling catches with the reported increase in the stock, and their protests were disregarded. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4.1; Date: early to mid-1980s.

Q7. [paraphrase] The inshore fishers commissioned their own report (Report A, 1986). It highlighted the department's own downplayed retrospective analyses, which showed consistent and severe underestimation of fishing pressure since national control began. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4.1, citing Keats et al. 1986; Date: 1986.

Q8. "...we have consistently taken from 1.5–3 times the (20 % of stock) catch since 1977." — Source: Report A (Keats et al. 1986), as quoted in EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4.1; Date: 1986.

Q9. [paraphrase] The fisheries department dismissed Report A "as biased pseudoscience written to support a political agenda". — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4.1, citing Finlayson 1994 (reporting the department's response); Date: 1986.

Q10. [paraphrase] Report A gained media attention, which forced the federal fisheries minister to commission an official report. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4.1; Date: 1986–1987.

Q11. [paraphrase] In 1988 the department claimed a "five fold increase" in the stock since 1976. Its management was widely held up as an example of how cautious, science-driven management could turn around a seemingly hopeless situation. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4; Date: 1988.

Q12. [paraphrase] The official report (Report B, 1988), prepared by fisheries scientists, concluded that the stock had grown since 1977, though "probably only very slowly" after 1982. It also stated that "fishing mortality actually exerted has been considerably in excess of target mortality" because of "consistent overestimation of the current stock size". — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4.1, quoting Alverson et al. 1988; Date: 1988.

Q13. [paraphrase] Report B explained that about five years of later data were needed before estimates of fishing mortality and biomass for any given year converged on the correct answer. Over shorter periods the estimates largely reflected the assumed fishing mortality, a critical flaw when a stock depends on the last few years' breeding success. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4.1; Date: 1988.

Q14. [paraphrase] Report B's executive summary reversed the emphasis of its body. It stated that the department's fishing-mortality calculations fell "within the range of estimates supported by the data", albeit at the lower end. The department controlled the report's release. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4.1; Date: 1988.

Q15. [paraphrase] The department's public response stressed that "the difference in numbers overall was about 4 to 5 percent" and that "the credibility of [the department's] science was not questioned". Privately the report's authors were "less sanguine", and the department reappraised its methods internally. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4.1; Date: 1988.

Q16. [paraphrase] The department's 1989 assessment assumed higher fishing mortality, concluded that the stock was not growing, and recommended that the offshore catch be virtually halved. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4.1; Date: 1989.

Q17. [paraphrase] The reappraisal was seen as an admission that the department had been wrong all along. This was a serious problem for the administration, which relied on "science" as the arbiter of competing claims to the resource. Offshore fishers now complained bitterly that there was no evidence the stock had fallen. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4.1; Date: 1989.

Q18. [paraphrase] The minister called a third enquiry (Report C, 1990), fully independent of the department. It concluded that before 1989 fishing mortality had probably been more than double the intended level and the stock little more than half its assumed size. The stock had therefore been fished at levels pointing towards commercial extinction, a conclusion widely reported in the media. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4.1, citing Harris 1990; Date: 1990.

Q19. "We acted in substantial ignorance of the animals in which we were principally interested and in almost total ignorance of the dynamics of the ecosystems in which they existed." — Source: Report C (Harris 1990), as quoted in EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4.1; Date: 1990.

Q20. "We continued for too long to wear rose tinted glasses and to interpret all data in the manner best calculated to support and confirm the model of growth upon which our hearts had been set." — Source: Report C (Harris 1990), as quoted in EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4.1; Date: 1990.

Q21. [paraphrase] Report C found that what was believed to be the best available theory, data and methods "will legitimately support claims of stock status ranging from sustainable growth to dangerous decline". — Source: Report C (Harris 1990), as quoted in EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4.1; Date: 1990.

Q22. [paraphrase] Report C estimated that bringing removals into line with the 20% goal would require cutting the allowable catch from 235,000 tonnes (1989) to about 125,000 tonnes. It said this "would precipitate social and economic repercussions of a particularly drastic nature". — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4.2, citing Harris 1990; Date: 1990.

Q23. [paraphrase] Report C likened fisheries science to the Ptolemaic model of the solar system: when observations did not fit the theory, another layer of complexity was added instead of questioning the basic theory. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.4.3, citing Harris 1990; Date: 1990.

Q24. [paraphrase] In an earlier regional fishery for a small pelagic species, state scientists warned from the mid-1920s of depletion and an "imperative" need to cut catches. In 1939 they were replaced by appointees who reported "no reason to be concerned", and the stock collapsed in 1942. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.3, citing McEvoy 1986; Date: 1920s–1942.

Q25. [paraphrase] After that collapse the processing firms moved their equipment to another continent and helped open a new fishery, which followed the same model and collapsed in the early 1970s. A major herring stock in another sea was brought to the brink of collapse by the 1970s. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.3.2 and Annex 2 summary, citing McEvoy 1986; Date: 1940s–1970s.

Q26. [paraphrase] Official enquiries into overfishing were held in one European state between 1866 and 1893. An 1865 observer recorded that catches per boat fell while the area of net carried per boat nearly quadrupled. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 2, §2.2, citing Bertram 1865 and Reports 1866 and 1885; Date: 1865–1893.
