# Evidence packet K18

**Label:** X-rays and radioactive materials used in medicine and research.
**De-identification:** Not feasible; the agent is named. Coders may know the outcome. Score only from the items below.
**t0:** 1896, the first reports of injury from X-ray exposure.
**Scoring point:** t0 + 5 = 1901. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

## Evidence

Q1. [paraphrase] Roentgen publishes the first account of producing X-rays and immediately recognises their diagnostic value. He publicises the work by sending scientists an X-ray image of his wife's hand. — Source: Roentgen 1895, as described in EEA 2001, ch. 3 §3.1; Date: 1895

Q2. [paraphrase] "There was an immediate worldwide interest in this tool for medical diagnosis". Physicians generally took a "cavalier approach". The scientific and lay worlds were "captivated" by the new phenomenon. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 3 §3.1, describing 1896; Date: 1896

Q3. [paraphrase] Thomas Edison, Nikola Tesla and Emil Grubbe note eye and skin injuries from X-ray exposure. Edison cautions against excessive exposure. — Source: Edison, "Effect of X-rays upon the eye", Nature 53:421 (1896), cited in EEA 2001, ch. 3; Date: 1896

Q4. [paraphrase] Grubbe suffers X-ray burns and dermatitis within about a month of the discovery. — Source: review of early X-ray victims (PMC3520298), reporting 1896 events; Date: 1896

Q5. [paraphrase] At Vanderbilt, Daniel X-rays a colleague's head for one hour as a trial before locating a bullet in a child's head. The colleague's hair falls out under the tube 21 days later. Daniel reports it in a letter to *Science*. — Source: Daniel, "The X-rays", Science 3:562–563 (1896); Vanderbilt News retrospective; Date: 1896

Q6. [paraphrase] A physicist deliberately exposes his little finger to the direct beam for several days. The severe damage leads him to caution against overexposure, "...or there may be cause for regret when too late". — Source: Thomson 1896, quoted in EEA 2001, ch. 3; Date: 1896

Q7. [paraphrase] A Columbia graduate giving X-ray demonstrations suffers severe burns to his hands and chest. — Source: review of early X-ray victims (PMC3520298), reporting 1896 events; Date: 1896

Q8. [paraphrase] A British medical report describes "injurious effects on the skin" from X-rays. — Source: Stevens, BMJ 1:998 (1896) (title and citation only; text not reached); Date: 1896

Q9. [paraphrase] A Chicago practitioner recommends keeping X-ray exposures as short as possible, keeping the tube at least 30 cm from the body, and coating the skin with petroleum jelly beforehand. — Source: review of early X-ray victims (PMC3520298), reporting December 1896; Date: 1896

Q10. [paraphrase] Partly because of the growing number of injury reports, some physicians see a possible therapeutic use. The first "treatment" is reported in Chicago in 1896, on a woman with advanced breast carcinoma. — Source: Stone 1946, as described in EEA 2001, ch. 3; Date: 1896

Q11. [paraphrase] "By the late 1890s there were numerous reports of radiation skin burns and loss of hair (epilation) in the scientific literature." — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 3 §3.1; Date: 1899

Q12. [paraphrase] Despite occasional "ominous" warnings, the general consensus is that X-rays used judiciously are without adverse effects. It is assumed that harm cannot come from an agent the senses cannot perceive. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 3 (citing Thompson 1898); Date: 1898

Q13. [paraphrase] Theories of the time attribute the injuries not to the X-rays themselves but to static electricity or individual sensitivity. One author denies outright that X-ray effects exist. — Source: Scott 1897, as described in EEA 2001, ch. 3; Date: 1897

Q14. [paraphrase] A degree of overconfidence about X-ray use exists in the medical profession, despite the reports of adverse effects and the therapeutic use of X-rays. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 3, describing the late 1890s; Date: 1897

Q15. [paraphrase] A New York journalist campaigns for state licensing of radiologists and radiographers, and suggests that injuring a patient should be a criminal act. — Source: Dennis, "The roentgen energy today", Dental Cosmos 41:853 (1899), cited in EEA 2001, ch. 3; Date: 1899

Q16. [paraphrase] With early tubes operating at low voltages, exposures of more than an hour are common. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 3 §3.1; Date: 1896–1901

Q17. [paraphrase] There is no agreed unit of radiation exposure or dose, which is a problem for anyone trying to set protective limits. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 3 §3.1; Date: 1896–1901

Q18. [paraphrase] Within weeks of Roentgen's work, Becquerel discovers radioactivity. In 1898 the Curies report the discovery of radium. The hazard of radioactivity is "no more recognised than that of X-rays". — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 3 §3.2; Date: 1896–1898

Q19. [paraphrase] Becquerel and Pierre Curie suffer skin erythema from carrying radioactive samples in their pockets. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 3 §3.2; Date: c. 1900–1901

Q20. [paraphrase] From 1900, a Boston dentist and physician begins publishing many papers urging physicians to use the minimum possible exposure. He proposes shielding and collimating tubes, and a "tolerance" test: shielding is adequate if a photographic plate outside the tube does not fog in 7 minutes. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 3 §3.1 (Rollins; EEA gives the publication period as 1900–1904); Date: 1900

Q21. [paraphrase] Media and public excitement and "often inappropriate" publicity mean that damage to health, especially in the long term, is "not given any prominence". The diagnostic and therapeutic value of X-rays means "caution tended to be thrown away". — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 3 introduction, describing the period after discovery; Date: 1896
