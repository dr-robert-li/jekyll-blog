# Evidence packet K21

- **Label:** A new transmissible brain disease in cattle, spread through feed made from rendered animal remains.
- **De-identification:** Not feasible without losing meaning. The disease, its relation to a sheep disease and a human disease, and the national setting are what the evidence is about, so names are kept. Coders may know the outcome; score only from the items below.
- **t0:** 1986. EEA (2001, Table A2.1 in EEA 2013 Annex 2; ch. 15, Table 15.1) gives the first early warning as the range "1979–1986". 1986 is the first warning about this disease itself (first cases officially acknowledged). The 1979 warning about pathogens in rendered feed is included below as background.
- **Scoring point:** t0 + 5 = 1991. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

## Evidence

Q1. [paraphrase] Rendered slaughterhouse wastes had been recycled into animal feed since at least the early 20th century. The known risks of the practice included the transmission, recycling and amplification of pathogens. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, Box 15.1, citing Cooke 1998; Date: pre-1986 (long-standing practice).

Q2. [paraphrase] In 1979 the UK Royal Commission on Environmental Pollution recognised the risk of pathogens in animal feed and recommended minimum processing standards for the rendering industry. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, Box 15.1 and Table 15.1, citing RCEP 1979; Date: 1979.

Q3. [paraphrase] The incoming government withdrew the proposed rendering standards as "unnecessary and excessively restrictive" and indicated that industry should decide for itself how to operate its equipment. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, Box 15.1, citing Barclay 1996; Date: 1979–1980.

Q4. [paraphrase] In the mid-1970s the US Department of Agriculture excluded carcasses of scrapie-affected or scrapie-exposed sheep and goats from human and animal food. This was partly because of concern about a possible link between scrapie and Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD). No similar step was taken in the UK. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, Box 15.1, citing Martin 1998; Date: mid-1970s.

Q5. [paraphrase] The first cases of the new cattle disease were officially acknowledged in November 1986. Its pathology closely resembled scrapie, a transmissible spongiform encephalopathy (TSE) endemic in UK sheep. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.2; Date: 1986.

Q6. [paraphrase] TSEs are poorly understood, untreatable and invariably fatal brain diseases of animals and humans. CJD is the best-known human TSE. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.2; Date: 1986 (state of knowledge).

Q7. [paraphrase] Ministry scientists suspected that the cattle disease had come from scrapie-infected sheep and was being spread through contaminated feed. Contaminated feed was quickly confirmed as the principal vector. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.2; Date: 1987–1988.

Q8. [paraphrase] In the late 1980s no test could reliably detect the pathogen in live animals before clinical signs appeared. Infected but symptom-free cattle could not be told apart from uninfected cattle. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.2; Date: late 1980s.

Q9. [paraphrase] Experiments showed that the host range of a scrapie strain could not be predicted once it had jumped to another species. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.2, citing Kimberlin et al. 1987; Date: 1987.

Q10. [paraphrase] Veterinary officials in the agriculture ministry recognised a possible risk to human health as soon as the disease was diagnosed, but judged the probability "acceptably slight". — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, Box 15.1 and §15.2, citing BSE Inquiry 1999b; Date: 1986.

Q11. [paraphrase] One ministry, the Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries and Food (MAFF), was responsible both for promoting the economic interests of farmers and the food industry and for protecting the public from food-borne hazards. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.1; Date: 1986 (institutional arrangement).

Q12. "...in December 1986 when recognition of the disease began to crystallise, we were at the Central Veterinary Laboratory placed under strict confidentiality as to discussing it with outside people..." This is a ministry scientist recalling events. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.3, quoting BSE Inquiry 1999b, p. 13; Date: 1986.

Q13. [paraphrase] In the first half of 1987 there was "a policy of restricting, even within the State Veterinary Service, the dissemination of any information about the new disease". Most of the research community, the medical profession and officials in other departments did not learn of the disease until early 1988. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.3, quoting Phillips et al. 2000, vol. 3, para. 2.137; Date: 1987–1988.

Q14. "...the disorder could have potentially serious implications, not only domestically but for UK exports." The Chief Veterinary Officer said this when first briefing the minister. He advised against regulatory restrictions and warned that publicity "might lead to hysterical demands for immediate, draconian Government measures and might also lead other countries to reject UK exports of live cattle and bovine embryos and semen". — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.3, quoting BSE Inquiry 1999b, pp. 27–28; Date: 1987.

Q15. [paraphrase] Making the disease notifiable was rejected in part because, in one official's words, it "might imply to the general public we know something they don't, like the meat or milk is a source of danger for humans". — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.3, quoting Phillips et al. 2000, vol. 3, para. 2.130; Date: 1987.

Q16. [paraphrase] About 1,200 clinical cases were recorded in 1987 and the first half of 1988. The disease was not notifiable, so true incidence was almost certainly higher, and most affected animals were sold as human food. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.3; Date: 1987–1988.

Q17. "...we do not know whether (BSE) can be passed to humans... There is no evidence that people can be infected, but we cannot say there is no risk... we have to face up to the possibility that the disease could cross another species gap." This is the ministry's Under Secretary for Animal Health. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.2, quoting BSE Inquiry 1999c, para. 59; Date: early 1988.

Q18. [paraphrase] In February 1988, with media attention growing, senior officials privately recommended a slaughter-and-compensation policy for clinically diseased cattle, arguing that the government would be held responsible if the disease later proved transmissible to humans. The minister rejected this. His private secretary cited the lack of "offsetting savings" and said such action "would make the export position much worse, not better". — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.3, quoting a 1988 ministerial minute; Date: 1988.

Q19. [paraphrase] The Chief Medical Officer at the Department of Health was first told about the disease in March 1988, 17 months after the agriculture ministry was alerted. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.4, citing BSE Inquiry 1999c, para. 115; Date: 1988.

Q20. "...by analogy (with scrapie and CJD) BSE may be transmissible to humans." This is from the minutes of a meeting at the National Institute for Biological Standards and Control attended by senior government advisers. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, Box 15.1, quoting BSE Inquiry 1999c, para. 186; Date: May 1988.

Q21. [paraphrase] Most rendered animal protein went to pig and poultry feed, which was the rendering industry's principal market. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.4, citing BSE Inquiry transcript, 29 June 1998; Date: 1988.

Q22. "...the most we could say is that any ruminant protein fed to (pigs) might contain the agent of BSE or scrapie. Whether or not infection would be established in the pig and whether it might replicate is unknown." The Chief Veterinary Officer said this privately to a colleague. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.4, quoting BSE Inquiry transcript, 16 June 1998, p. 99; Date: June 1988.

Q23. [paraphrase] Scrapie was known to pass from ewes to lambs. The ministry did not start funding research into maternal transmission of the cattle disease until 1989. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.4, citing Barclay 1996 and *Nature* 1990; Date: 1989.

Q24. [paraphrase] At a September 1989 ministry meeting, several people consulted raised the possible danger of mechanically recovered meat, which contains residual nervous tissue. The minutes record "an expression of the illogicality of what was being done" and that "It was agreed not to raise it". — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.5, quoting BSE Inquiry 1999e, para. 263; Date: 1989.

Q25. [paraphrase] UK exports of meat and bone meal to other EU states rose from 12,553 tonnes in 1988 to 25,005 tonnes in 1989. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, Box 15.2, citing European Parliament 1997, p. 8; Date: 1988–1989.

Q26. [paraphrase] In summer 1989 the European Commission asked the UK to stop exporting such feed, and the UK refused. The Commission said it lacked the legal basis to ban the exports itself and invited member states to ban imports nationally, which some did. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, Box 15.2, citing BSE Inquiry 1999d, para. 257, and European Parliament 1996; Date: 1989.

Q27. [paraphrase] In February 1990 the environmental health officers who enforce slaughterhouse controls told the ministry that existing methods of removing cattle brains contaminated head meat. A senior official advised the food minister against new controls. He said they "would fuel debate on BSE generally and, inevitably, lead to demands for similar action on spinal cords", and that a measure on spinal columns "would have grave consequences for the industry and for the export trade". — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.5, quoting BSE Inquiry 1999f, p. 7; Date: 1990.

Q28. [paraphrase] From 1990 the disease was found to be transmissible through food to domestic cats, a species not susceptible to scrapie. This suggested that its host range differed from scrapie's and was unpredictable. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, Box 15.1; Date: 1990.

Q29. "...in the present state of knowledge, it would not be justified to state categorically that there was no risk to humans, and it was not appropriate to insist on a zero risk." This is the government's Spongiform Encephalopathy Advisory Committee. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.5, quoting BSE Inquiry transcript, 24 March 1998, p. 71; Date: May 1990.

Q30. "...clear scientific evidence that British beef is perfectly safe." The Agriculture Minister said this in the House of Commons. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 15, §15.5, quoting Hansard 1990, col. 906; Date: 7 June 1990.
