# Evidence packet K11

- **Label:** Catastrophic (beyond-design-basis) accidents at civil nuclear power reactors, with transboundary radioactive release.
- **De-identification:** Not feasible. The defining accident cannot be described without naming it. Coders may know later events, including a second major accident in 2011. Score only from the items below.
- **t0:** 1986. EEA's chapter treats the April 1986 accident as the first warning in the series ("late lessons from Chernobyl, early warnings from Fukushima"). The hazard coded here is the risk of catastrophic reactor accidents across the reactor fleet, not the single event.
- **Scoring point:** t0 + 5 = 1991. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

## Evidence

Q1. [paraphrase] On 26 April 1986 an explosion destroyed Unit 4 of the Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant in northern Ukraine. What is understood to have been a misconceived reactor experiment led, through a positive void coefficient, to a power excursion and a steam explosion. — Source: EEA 2013 *Late lessons II* ch. 18 §18.2; Date: 1986

Q2. [paraphrase] Over six days of open containment, 30–60% of the reactor core's fission products (6.7 tonnes of core material) were released and carried over more than 200 000 km² of Europe. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 18 §18.2 (citing UNDP 2002); Date: 1986

Q3. [paraphrase] Authorities evacuated and later relocated about 115 000 people from around the reactor in 1986. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 18 §18.2 (citing UNSCEAR 2008); Date: 1986

Q4. [paraphrase] The release was first detected outside the USSR on 28 April 1986, when workers at Sweden's Forsmark nuclear plant set off radiation alarms. Swedish authorities then traced the source to the Soviet Union, before any Soviet announcement. — Source: IAEA, *Summary Report on the Post-Accident Review Meeting on the Chernobyl Accident* (INSAG-1), 1986; contemporary press; Date: 1986

Q5. [paraphrase] Information was controlled inside the USSR. The first official statement was brief, and public May Day events in Kiev went ahead on 1 May 1986 while radioactive material was being deposited in the region. — Source: contemporary press accounts; Date: 1986

Q6. [paraphrase] At the IAEA post-accident review meeting (Vienna, August 1986) the Soviet delegation attributed the accident mainly to operators violating procedures. The IAEA safety advisory group's report (INSAG-1) adopted this emphasis, while noting design features that contributed. — Source: IAEA INSAG-1 (Safety Series 75), 1986; Date: 1986

Q7. [paraphrase] The IAEA's statute gives it a dual mandate: to "accelerate and enlarge the contribution of atomic energy to peace, health and prosperity" and to apply safety standards. — Source: Statute of the IAEA, Art. II (1957); Date: 1957

Q8. [paraphrase] Fallout was measured in milk, leafy vegetables, game and reindeer meat in Scandinavia and central Europe, far from the plant. — Source: national radiation-protection monitoring reports in Sweden, Norway and Germany; Date: 1986–1987

Q9. [paraphrase] Major accidents had happened before. The 1957 Windscale reactor fire in the UK released radioactive iodine, and milk from the surrounding area was discarded. The 1957 Kyshtym waste explosion in the USSR was kept secret for decades. The 1979 Three Mile Island partial core melt in the US released little radioactivity. — Source: UK Atomic Energy Authority Penney report (1957); US Kemeny Commission (1979); Medvedev, *Nuclear Disaster in the Urals* (1979); Date: 1957–1979

Q10. [paraphrase] The US presidential commission on Three Mile Island concluded that the fundamental problems were people-related, in the organisations, procedures and practices around the reactor, rather than equipment alone. — Source: Report of the President's Commission on the Accident at Three Mile Island (Kemeny Commission), 1979; Date: 1979

Q11. [paraphrase] A 1975 US Reactor Safety Study (WASH-1400) estimated very low probabilities of a core-melt accident. A 1978 review panel found its uncertainty bounds understated, and in 1979 the US regulator withdrew its endorsement of the study's executive summary. — Source: US NRC, WASH-1400 (1975); Lewis Committee report NUREG/CR-0400 (1978); NRC policy statement (January 1979); Date: 1975–1979

Q12. [paraphrase] A sociological analysis argued that in interactively complex, tightly coupled technologies such as nuclear plants, multiple unexpected failures will interact, so serious accidents are "normal" and cannot be designed out. — Source: Perrow, *Normal Accidents* (1984), cited in EEA 2013 ch. 18 §18.5; Date: 1984

Q13. [paraphrase] Probabilistic risk assessment underpins the concept of acceptable risk in nuclear regulation. It assumes independent failures and enumerated reactor states, which tends to under-count cascading accident scenarios. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 18 §18.5 (describing the established method); Date: method in use from 1975

Q14. [paraphrase] Operators' liability for accident damage was capped by international conventions (the Paris Convention 1960 and Brussels Supplementary Convention 1963) and in the US by the Price-Anderson Act (1957). Ordinary property insurance policies excluded nuclear damage. — Source: Paris Convention on Third Party Liability in the Field of Nuclear Energy (1960); Brussels Supplementary Convention (1963); Price-Anderson Act (1957); EEA 2013 ch. 18 §18.4; Date: 1957–1963

Q15. [paraphrase] A financial cap on operators' liability may weaken incentives for prevention and favour nuclear over other energy sources. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 18 §18.4 (economic argument, citing Faure and Fiore); Date: argument applies to the caps of 1957–1963

Q16. [paraphrase] Nuclear power was a large share of electricity in several countries. In France about 70% of electricity came from reactors built and run by a single state-owned utility. Reactors were supplied by a small number of vendors. — Source: IAEA Power Reactor Information System; national energy statistics; Date: 1986

Q17. [paraphrase] About 400 power reactors were operating worldwide, and many governments had plans and sunk investment in further construction. — Source: IAEA Power Reactor Information System; Date: 1986

Q18. [paraphrase] Opinion surveys in several Western European countries recorded marked increases in opposition to nuclear power after the accident. — Source: contemporary national and European opinion surveys; Date: 1986–1987

Q19. [paraphrase] Radioactive iodine was known to concentrate in the thyroid, and children's thyroids were known to be especially sensitive. Thyroid cancers had followed childhood exposure in earlier settings, such as Marshall Islanders exposed to 1954 bomb-test fallout and children given X-ray therapy. — Source: Conard et al. on the Marshall Islands (*JAMA*, 1970s–1980); thyroid radiation literature cited in EEA 2013 ch. 18 (e.g. Larsen et al. 1982); Date: 1954–1982

Q20. [paraphrase] Projections of eventual excess cancer deaths from the accident's collective dose varied by more than an order of magnitude depending on the dose-response model, especially on whether a linear no-threshold model was applied to low doses spread across Europe. — Source: Anspaugh, Catlin and Goldman, *Science* 242:1513–1519 (1988); EEA 2013 ch. 18 Box 18.1 (model uncertainty); Date: 1988

Q21. [paraphrase] Evidence for radiation risk at the time rested largely on the Japanese atomic-bomb survivor cohort, a high-dose, single-exposure population. Extrapolating to chronic low doses was uncertain. — Source: Shimizu, Kato and Schull 1991 (Life Span Study), cited in EEA 2013 ch. 18; Box 18.1; Date: 1991

Q22. [paraphrase] An IAEA-led international assessment of the affected areas reported no health disorders that could be attributed directly to radiation exposure. It found the official intervention levels generally conservative, and attributed significant psychological effects to anxiety. Its conclusions were disputed by local scientists and officials. — Source: IAEA, *The International Chernobyl Project: Technical Report* (1991); Date: 1991

Q23. [paraphrase] Most of the harm fell on residents of Belarus, Ukraine and Russia and on other European countries downwind, who had no part in the reactor's design, siting or operation. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 18 §18.2 (cross-boundary pollution and relocation); Date: 1986

Q24. [paraphrase] The harm chain runs from a reactor failure through atmospheric release, deposition, food-chain transfer and internal dose to cancers that appear after years to decades of latency. Acute effects were confined to plant staff and emergency workers. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 18 §18.2 and Box 18.1; Date: 1986 (mechanism)
