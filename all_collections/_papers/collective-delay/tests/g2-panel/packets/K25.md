# Evidence packet K25

- **Label:** Chemical Z, a high-volume monomer used to make a clear hard plastic and can coatings, with hormone-like (oestrogenic) activity.
- **De-identification:** Partly feasible. The chemical and the plastic are given neutral labels. Coders may recognise the case from its history and may know the outcome; score only from the items below.
- **t0:** 1993. EEA (2013, ch. 10) has no early-warnings table. Its section on identifying the risk says the risks "were re-discovered" in 1993, when the substance was found leaching from plastic labware (§10.3). The 1936 finding of weak oestrogenic activity (§10.1) predates the plastic, so it is included only as prior evidence.
- **Scoring point:** t0 + 5 = 1998. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

## Evidence

Q1. [paraphrase] Two British researchers searching for cheap synthetic substitutes for natural oestrogen in medicine identified Chemical Z as a weak functional oestrogen in rat test systems. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.1, citing Dodds and Lawson 1936, 1938; Date: 1936.

Q2. [paraphrase] The same team then discovered a much more potent synthetic oestrogen, which was later used as a pharmaceutical and showed severe side effects. Chemical Z was not developed as a medicine. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.1, citing Dodds et al. 1938 and Meyers 1983; Date: 1938.

Q3. [paraphrase] Chemical Z was marketed as an industrial chemical. In 1957 it was polymerised to make a clear, hard plastic (Plastic P), which was seen as a significant advance. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.1; Date: 1957.

Q4. [paraphrase] When Plastic P was introduced, neither non-monotonic dose responses nor the harm from foetal exposure to synthetic oestrogens were known or commonly recognised. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.6; Date: 1957.

Q5. [paraphrase] Endocrinologists described inverted U-shaped dose-response curves for hormone-like agents: very low doses have a small effect, intermediate doses the largest effect, and high doses little or none. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.4, citing Sonnenschein et al. 1989; Date: 1989.

Q6. [paraphrase] An EU directive listed Chemical Z among substances allowed in food-contact plastics, with a specific migration limit of 3 mg per kg of food. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.2, citing European Commission 1990 (Directive 90/128/EEC); Date: 1990.

Q7. [paraphrase] At a 1991 scientific conference the phrase "endocrine disruptor" was used for the first time. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.3, citing Markey et al. 2002; Date: 1991.

Q8. [paraphrase] The first scientific paper using the term described signs of hormonal disruption in wildlife and humans and mainly suspected pesticides. Chemical Z was added to the list of potential endocrine disruptors a year later. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.3, citing Bason and Colborn 1992; Date: 1992–1993.

Q9. [paraphrase] A university endocrinology team found an unknown oestrogenic substance contaminating their assays. They identified it as Chemical Z leaching from polycarbonate culture flasks during autoclaving. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.3, citing Krishnan et al. 1993, *Endocrinology*; Date: 1993.

Q10. [paraphrase] No government or industry risk-identification programme had flagged Chemical Z as hormonally active, although such programmes had run in Europe since 1982 with considerable government and industry input. The finding was accidental. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.3; Date: 1982–1993.

Q11. [paraphrase] Several European chemical companies had decades of hormone research but did not take part in identifying or assessing environmental chemicals with hormone-disrupting properties. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.3; Date: 1982–1993.

Q12. [paraphrase] An academic group analysed the liquid from canned vegetables and found oestrogenic activity released from the lacquer coatings of food cans. — Source: Brotons et al. 1995, *Environmental Health Perspectives* 103(6):608–612 (EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.2 cites it as Brotons et al. 1994); Date: 1995.

Q13. [paraphrase] In 1995 workshops in four countries discussed the emerging issue of hormonally active environmental chemicals. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.3; Date: 1995.

Q14. [paraphrase] A 1996 book on endocrine disruptors, with a foreword by a former national vice-president, put the issue on the global political agenda. In the second half of the 1990s Chemical Z became the most prominent example of an endocrine disruptor in scientific and public debate. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.3, citing Colborn, Dumanoski and Myers 1996; Date: 1996–1998.

Q15. [paraphrase] It was assumed that the monomer would not be released from Plastic P. Together with its low hormonal potency (1,000–10,000 times weaker than oestradiol), this meant there was "no real concern" about human exposure and toxicity. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.6, citing Biles et al. 1997; Date: 1997.

Q16. [paraphrase] Researchers measured Chemical Z in reusable polycarbonate food-contact plastics and its migration into food-simulating liquids. — Source: Biles et al. 1997 (as cited in EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.6); Date: 1997.

Q17. [paraphrase] A low dose of Chemical Z caused proliferation in breast tissue, and it was much more potent than its oestrogen-receptor binding predicted. Science "reacted immediately", and many papers on low-dose effects followed. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.4, citing Colerangle and Roy 1997; Date: 1997.

Q18. [paraphrase] When mothers were dosed during pregnancy and lactation, Chemical Z interfered with normal sperm production, reducing sperm numbers later in life in rodent offspring. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.5, citing vom Saal et al. 1998; Date: 1998.

Q19. [paraphrase] In vitro tests showed that Chemical Z competes with oestradiol for the alpha and beta oestrogen receptors, with relative binding affinities at least a thousand times lower than oestradiol's. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.5, citing Kuiper et al. 1998; Date: 1998.

Q20. [paraphrase] Toxicology rests on the principle that "the dose makes the poison": if a high dose does no harm, a low dose will not either. Hormone-like agents with non-monotonic dose responses challenge that principle. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.4 (Paracelsus 1539; inverted-U curves reported from 1989); Date: 1989–1998.

Q21. [paraphrase] With hormone-like agents the organism is most sensitive during development, but effects are often seen only much later. Effects of exposure of a foetus or newborn on sperm may not be measurable until puberty, more than a decade later. By the time effects appear, the exposure has gone, which makes it extremely hard to link cause and effect in humans by epidemiology. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.5 (reasoning applied to the 1998 rodent findings); Date: 1998.

Q22. [paraphrase] Prenatal exposure to the potent synthetic oestrogen mentioned in Q2 had already been linked to harm that appeared in the exposed offspring years later. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.1 and §10.5 (DES side effects; Meyers 1983); Date: 1983.

Q23. [paraphrase] Chemical Z is hard to analyse because it leaches easily from plastic laboratory equipment and contaminates samples. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 10, §10.2; Date: 1993 (the contamination problem behind Q9).
