# Evidence packet K15

- **Label:** Tetraethyl lead (TEL) as an anti-knock additive in motor petrol.
- **De-identification:** Not feasible. The agent, the firms and the 1925 hearing are named in every source and cannot be removed without losing meaning. Coders may know the outcome. Score only from the items below.
- **t0:** 1925. EEA dates the early warning to the May 1925 Surgeon General's conference (the "one day trial"), where public health experts warned of long-term low-level poisoning of the public.
- **Scoring point:** t0 + 5 = 1930. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

## Evidence

Q1. [paraphrase] In 1922 a chemistry professor who knew TEL from wartime evaluation as a possible weapon warned the US Public Health Service (PHS) of a "serious menace to the public health". He reasoned that on busy streets "it is highly probable that the lead oxide dust will remain in the lower stratum." — Source: Mansfield Clark 1922, as quoted in EEA 2013 *Late lessons II* ch. 3 §3.3.2; Date: 1922

Q2. The Surgeon General wrote to the chair of General Motors' board asking whether the public health effects had been considered, "since lead poisoning in human beings is of the cumulative type resulting frequently from the daily intake of minute quantities." — Source: Cummings 1922, as quoted in EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.3.2 (citing Needleman 1997); Date: 1922

Q3. The developer of the additive replied that the question had been given "very serious consideration ... although no actual experimental data has been taken". He was confident that "the average street will probably be so free from lead that it will be impossible to detect it or its absorption." — Source: Midgley 1922, as quoted in EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.3.2; Date: 1922

Q4. [paraphrase] In 1924 General Motors, DuPont and Standard Oil jointly created the Ethyl Corporation of America to produce TEL. The chair of GM's board and the head of DuPont were brothers, and both firms had close links with Standard Oil. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.3.1; Date: 1924

Q5. [paraphrase] The company decided at the outset that the word "lead" would not appear in its name or sales literature. The little-known term "ethyl" was used instead "so as not to alarm the public". — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.3.1; Date: 1924

Q6. [paraphrase] GM paid the US Bureau of Mines to run animal experiments under conditions set by the Ethyl Corporation. "Lead" was to be replaced by "ethyl" even in internal correspondence, and draft reports went to Ethyl for "comments, criticism and approval" before publication. The Bureau's chief chemist objected and was overruled. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.3.1 (citing Needleman 1997); Date: 1923–1924

Q7. [paraphrase] A Yale professor of physiology turned down GM's invitation to study TEL and declined to join the Bureau of Mines investigation. He called GM funding of the work "extremely unfortunate" given the "urgent need for an absolutely unbiased investigation". His long-standing contracts with the Bureau of Mines and Standard Oil were then terminated. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.3.2; Date: 1922–1924

Q8. [paraphrase] In late October 1924 five workers at Standard Oil's TEL laboratory at Bayway, New Jersey, died within days and 35 others showed severe neurological symptoms. At least six other workers died at the DuPont plant at Deepwater and at GM's Dayton laboratory. Journalists found more than 300 cases of lead poisoning at Deepwater. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 3 Box 3.5; Date: 1924

Q9. [paraphrase] Newspapers ran headlines such as "Mad gas claims third victim" and "Bar Ethyl gas as fifth victim dies", with pictures of workers in straitjackets. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.3.2; Date: 1924

Q10. [paraphrase] The day after the fifth death, the Bureau of Mines published a report saying animal studies showed no risk to the public. On 1 November 1924 *The New York Times* headlined "No peril to public after long experiments with motor exhausts". — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.3.2; Date: 1924

Q11. [paraphrase] The editor of *The Journal of Industrial Hygiene*, the Dean of Harvard Medical School and others called the Bureau of Mines report "inadequate": too few animals, and exposures too short for reliable conclusions. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.3.2 (citing Drinker 1925); Date: 1925

Q12. [paraphrase] An unsigned editorial in *The American Journal of Public Health* said observational evidence had "corroborated the statement of complete safety". Its author, an Ohio health official, was also a paid consultant to the Ethyl Corporation and an adviser to the Bureau of Mines, which few people knew. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.3.2 (citing Ethyl Gasoline 1925; Hayhurst 1924); Date: 1924–1925

Q13. [paraphrase] Before the May 1925 conference the same consultant wrote privately to the PHS agreeing with a PHS scientist that "lead has no business in the human body". He added that "human progress cannot go on under such restrictions … if we are to survive among the nations." — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.4.1 (citing Hayhurst 1925); Date: 1925

Q14. [paraphrase] At the 24 May 1925 conference the industry made four points: leaded petrol was essential to American industrial progress; all innovation carries risk; the factory deaths came from workers' carelessness; and street exposure posed no public risk. An industry toxicologist spoke first, followed by eight other industry representatives, and together they took the morning session. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.4.1; Date: 1925

Q15. The Yale physiologist told the conference that lead was as serious a public health menace as infectious disease, and foresaw that "conditions will grow worse so gradually, and the development of lead poisoning will come on so insidiously ... that leaded petrol will be in nearly universal use ... before the public and the government awakens to the situation". — Source: USPHS 1925, as quoted in EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.4.1; Date: 1925

Q16. The leading US authority on industrial lead said there was no way to make leaded petrol safe by regulation: "You may control conditions within a factory ... but how can you control the whole country?" — Source: Hamilton 1925, as quoted in EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.4.1; Date: 1925

Q17. A physician who had treated the poisoned workers argued that "this ethyl gas is under suspicion and therefore should be withheld from public consumption until it is conclusively shown that it is not poisonous." — Source: Touart, at the 1925 PHS conference, as quoted in EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.4.1; Date: 1925

Q18. [paraphrase] An Ohio health official argued that 27 months of public use without reported poisonings showed leaded petrol was safe. A Columbia public health professor countered that deaths were an unsound indicator, because functional and mental disabilities among survivors also had to be measured. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.4.1; Date: 1925

Q19. The first president of the Ethyl Corporation told the conference that TEL, after a decade of research, was an "apparent gift of God". He asked: "Because some animals die and some do not die in some experiments, shall we give this thing up entirely?" — Source: Howard 1925, as quoted in EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.4.1; Date: 1925

Q20. [paraphrase] No alternative to TEL was discussed, although the Surgeon General had said he intended to spend two or three days on alternatives. The conference ended after less than seven hours. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.4.1 and Panel 3.1; Date: 1925

Q21. [paraphrase] The PHS expert committee then studied 252 garage and filling-station attendants, chauffeurs and factory workers over seven months. Its report said that "Owing to the incompleteness of the data, it is not possible to say definitely whether exposure to lead dust increases in garages", that "Longer exposure may show" harm, and that the "investigation must not be allowed to lapse." A recommendation by one member to keep searching for alternatives was left out of the final report. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.4.2; Date: 1925–1926

Q22. [paraphrase] Ethanol blends were a known alternative. US Geological Survey and Navy tests in 1907–08 found alcohol a cleaner fuel. In 1921 the additive's developer drove a car on a 30% alcohol blend to an engineers' meeting and praised alcohol's anti-knock properties. Alcohol was likely to be less profitable than a patentable additive. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.3.1 and Panel 3.1 (Kovarik); Date: 1907–1921

Q23. [paraphrase] Once they had invested in TEL production, the developers denied publicly that alternatives existed: "So far as science knows at the present time, tetraethyl lead is the only material available which can bring about these [antiknock] results". — Source: *The New York Times*, 7 April 1925, as quoted in EEA 2013 Panel 3.1; Date: 1925

Q24. [paraphrase] A US Department of Commerce report, published but not released a few days before the May 1925 conference, showed that alternative anti-knock additives (mostly ethanol blends) were in routine use in two dozen other industrial nations. — Source: EEA 2013 Panel 3.1; Date: 1925

Q25. [paraphrase] Lead was a long-recognised poison. Occupational lead poisoning was recorded in antiquity. In the 18th century Devon colic was traced to lead in cider presses, and the physician who showed it was condemned by local mill owners. In 1818 a statesman noted that "this mischievous effect from lead is at least 60 years old". The UK Potteries Regulations of 1867 controlled lead some 30 years after a manufacturer first asked for them. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.2 and Box 3.3; Date: 1768–1867

Q26. [paraphrase] Children poisoned by lead paint were reported in Australia (1892, 10 children) and the US (1914, a fatal case from a painted crib rail). By 1925 there was much evidence in the US, Europe and elsewhere that infants were poisoned by lead paint in their homes. — Source: EEA 2013 ch. 3 Box 3.4; Date: 1892–1925

Q27. [paraphrase] Chronic lead poisoning was described as developing "insidiously" years after exposure, damaging kidney or nervous system before symptoms appeared. — Source: Oliver 1911, as quoted in EEA 2013 ch. 3 §3.2; Date: 1911
