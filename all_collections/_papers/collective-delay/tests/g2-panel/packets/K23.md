# Evidence packet K23

- **Label:** Solvent X, used to apply a plastic lining inside drinking-water mains.
- **De-identification:** Partly feasible. The solvent, the manufacturer and the places are given neutral labels. Coders with toxicology knowledge may recognise the solvent from its history (an old anti-parasite drug, later a dry-cleaning and degreasing solvent) and may know the outcome; score only from the items below.
- **t0:** 1966. The EEA chapter's table (EEA 2013, ch. 4, Table 4.1) traces the solvent's toxicology from 1925. The first warning tied to this use is EEA's account of the first trial of the lined pipe in 1966, which "produced water with a slight chemical taste and odour" (ch. 4, §4.1). Earlier toxicology is included below as prior evidence.
- **Scoring point:** t0 + 5 = 1971. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

## Evidence

Q1. [paraphrase] In the early 1960s a city water-supply board looked for a replacement for cast-iron mains in low-flow areas, which had colour and taste problems. Manufacturer M, a maker of cement water mains, experimented with clear plastic linings. To apply a lining, the plastic was dissolved in Solvent X and the resulting slurry was painted inside the pipe. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.1; Date: early 1960s.

Q2. [paraphrase] The first trial of the lined pipe produced water with a slight chemical taste and odour. Routine water-quality tests (pH, alkalinity, hardness) did not reveal its source. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.1; Date: 1966.

Q3. [paraphrase] In early 1968 Manufacturer M delivered a pipe with the new lining to the water board for testing. Air trapped in the pipe had a chemical odour "similar to chloroform and strongly resembling a commercial dry-cleaning fluid used at a local cleaning plant". Under static conditions the lining kept adding a slight odour even after large volumes of water had passed through. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.1; Date: 1968.

Q4. [paraphrase] Company representatives were shown first-hand that the water retained a slight chloroform odour. At a later inspection of a newly installed pipe no odour was detected. The company explained the earlier odour by saying that the pipe had been covered in paper and had not "cured" completely. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.1; Date: 1968.

Q5. [paraphrase] There is no record that Manufacturer M investigated whether the solvent remained in the lining, or that any tests beyond routine water-quality measures, which do not identify organic contaminants, were done on water from the new pipe. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.1; Date: 1966–1971.

Q6. [paraphrase] The lined pipe was installed in regional distribution systems from 1969. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.1; Date: 1969.

Q7. [paraphrase] Solvent X and related chlorinated ethylenes were among the highest-volume chlorinated solvents of the century. They were used for dry cleaning, metal degreasing and printing, and as feedstocks for plastics and other chemicals. Solvent X had been used as a medicine against parasitic worms for almost a century. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.3; Date: 1920s–1960s.

Q8. [paraphrase] When the solvent was introduced as an anti-parasite drug, 3 of 55 dogs given what were believed to be therapeutic doses died, though none of them had received the largest doses. The researchers were the first of many to note large differences in individual susceptibility, and compared the drug's safety to carbon tetrachloride, a known liver toxin. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.4.1, citing Hall and Shillinger 1925; Date: 1925.

Q9. "...one cannot assume that any anthelmintic is entirely safe for human use until there are reliable reports on at least a million treatments without any untoward effects." This followed two cases of unconsciousness after a normal therapeutic dose. — Source: Sandground 1941, as quoted in EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.4.1; Date: 1941.

Q10. [paraphrase] Over decades of therapeutic use, the solvent caused serious harm in a small percentage of patients, some of it fatal at doses as small as 2–3 cc, and not necessarily related to dose. These side effects were tolerated because the parasitic disease was a major public-health problem. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.4.1; Date: 1925–1969.

Q11. [paraphrase] An industrial-health pioneer warned that data on animals might be hard to apply to humans and that low, chronic exposures can do more damage than large, acute ones. She cited lead and benzene as examples. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.4.2, citing Hamilton 1936, *New England Journal of Medicine*; Date: 1936.

Q12. "This, in my opinion, is a problem for the general practitioner since the use of these solvents in industry is increasing by leaps and bounds each day." — Source: Hamilton 1936, as quoted in EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.4.2; Date: 1936.

Q13. [paraphrase] A review of new volatile solvents noted that slow, chronic intoxications were hard to recognise. It listed the solvent's inhalation symptoms as nausea, giddiness, vomiting, mucous-membrane irritation, headache and drowsiness, and called for warnings on every container. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.4.2, citing St George 1937; Date: 1937.

Q14. [paraphrase] In rat experiments, kidney and liver changes appeared at 230 ppm. Human volunteers could smell the solvent at 50 ppm. The author concluded that a safe level for continuous exposure lay "somewhere between 100 ppm and 500 ppm" and that more human data were needed. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.4.2, citing Carpenter 1937; Date: 1937.

Q15. "This range of 100 to 500 ppm is in need of extensive study." — Source: Morse and Goldberg 1943, as quoted in EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.4.2; Date: 1943.

Q16. [paraphrase] An industrial toxicology textbook said the solvent should not be regarded as harmless, and that under some conditions it was more toxic than carbon tetrachloride, a recognised serious industrial hazard. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.4.2, citing Fairhall 1949; Date: 1949.

Q17. [paraphrase] A major manufacturer of the solvent began publishing toxicity reports. Its first report recommended limiting exposure to an average of 100 ppm and judged serious organ injury unlikely. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.4.2, citing Rowe et al. 1952 (manufacturer's in-house research); Date: 1952.

Q18. [paraphrase] At a small pump factory where parts were cleaned in 99% solvent at 200–400 ppm, a worker had severe liver cirrhosis, co-workers reported dizziness and intoxication, and three of seven workers tested had abnormal liver function. The authors urged more investigation of liver toxicity. "Thus, contemporary clinical observations contradicted the [manufacturer's] studies." — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.4.2, citing Coler and Rossmiller 1953; Date: 1953.

Q19. [paraphrase] An article titled "The dangers of [Solvent X]" noted that a toxic industrial chemical is often considered harmless until experience shows the opposite. It reviewed ten more poisoning cases, one of them fatal. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.4.2, citing Lob 1957; Date: 1957.

Q20. [paraphrase] The manufacturer's own researchers found that the solvent was excreted very slowly and accumulated with chronic exposure, and that liver tests might not become abnormal until two to three weeks after exposure. They also noted that the "mistaken perception" that the solvent was relatively non-toxic encouraged careless use. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.4.2, citing Stewart et al. 1961–1965 and Irish 1962; Date: 1961–1965.

Q21. [paraphrase] In a test of 27 industrial chemicals given to rats in pairs, four of the nine combinations with more-than-additive lethal effects contained Solvent X, more than any other chemical. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.4.2, citing Smyth et al. 1969; Date: 1969.

Q22. [paraphrase] A human exposure experiment by the manufacturer's researchers found an unexpected prevalence of light-headedness and abnormal neurological results at the lowest exposure levels. The authors could not interpret the finding. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.4.2, citing Stewart et al. 1970; Date: 1970.

Q23. [paraphrase] The 1968 national drinking-water standards stipulated that "drinking water should contain no impurity which would cause offence to the sense of sight, taste, or smell", to keep consumers from turning to less safe sources. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.5, citing USPHS 1968; Date: 1968.

Q24. [paraphrase] By the early 1950s, government agencies, professional bodies and trade associations publicly recognised that surface disposal of liquid wastes threatened groundwater. The propensity of chlorinated solvents to contaminate drinking water was generally understood in the 1940s–1960s. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.5, citing Colten 1991 and Amter and Ross 2001; Date: 1940s–1960s.

Q25. [paraphrase] Epidemiology was in its infancy. Evidence on solvent toxicity circulated mainly in specialist medical literature, and most treating physicians and workers did not know what materials they were exposed to. This kept solvent toxicity off the agenda and kept workers and unions out of the discussion. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 4, §4.5.1; Date: 1950s–1970.
