# Evidence packet K05
Label: A neurological disease among fishing families in coastal town Z near the chemical factory of Company C
De-identification: de-identified
t0 (first credible early warning): 1956
Scoring point: t0 + 5 = 1961. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

Names replaced: [Town Z] = the factory town; [Company C] = the chemical manufacturer; [Country J] = the country; [Prefecture P] = the regional government; [Regional University] = the regional medical school; [Industry Ministry], [Health Ministry] = national ministries; [Inland Sea] = the sea on which the town lies. "EEA case chapter" = EEA (2013) *Late lessons from early warnings* II, case chapter on this disease (Yorifuji, Tsuda & Harada). "MOE" = [Country J] Ministry of the Environment, *Lessons from [the disease] and Mercury Management* (2013). Note: informed coders may still recognise the case.

## Evidence
Q1. [paraphrase] Organic mercury was shown to be synthesised in producing acetaldehyde (Vogt & Nieuwland 1921); intoxication from occupational exposure to organic mercury was reported by Zangger (1930) and Koelsch (1937), and mercury poisoning occurred in German factories using acetaldehyde in 1930–37. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.1 and Table; Date: 1921–1937
Q2. [paraphrase] The [Company C] factory in [Town Z] began producing acetaldehyde from acetylene gas in 1932, using mercury as a catalyst. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.1; Date: 1932
Q3. [paraphrase] A researcher at the factory showed that organic mercury is synthesised in acetaldehyde production; whether the factory was aware of the toxic effects is unclear. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.1 (citing Arima 1979); Date: 1951
Q4. [paraphrase] The company paid the fishing cooperative small "sympathy money" for fishery damage in 1925–26 on condition no further complaints were made; another compensation contract followed in 1943; post-war negotiations in 1949 failed, the company calling the catch data "not scientific, not supported by data". — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.1 and Table; Date: 1925–1949
Q5. [paraphrase] The factory's acetaldehyde output, 9 159 t at its 1940 peak, quintupled to 45 200 t by 1960, 40% of [Country J]'s output; by 1959 the factory produced 85% of the country's octanol, a plastics ingredient previously imported. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.1; Date: 1940–1960
Q6. [paraphrase] [Country J] had run a trade deficit since the end of the Second World War, and plastic products were key exports helping to reduce it; the company was the most advanced chemical firm of the period. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.1; Date: 1945–1960
Q7. [paraphrase] The town grew with the factory to a population peak of 50 000 in 1956; at least 3 811 of its 19 819 workers were employed at the factory in 1960; the factory paid half the local taxes and provided public facilities, including a hospital. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.1; Date: 1956–1960
Q8. [paraphrase] The developer of the factory's acetaldehyde process managed the factory during the war and served as the town's mayor (1950–1958); the town was known as the company's "castle town". — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.1; Date: 1950–1958
Q9. [paraphrase] From about 1950 fishermen saw fish swimming as if crazed, sea birds unable to fly and shellfish rotting open on the beach; the cooperative's catch fell from an average of 459 225 kg (1950–53) to 172 305 kg in 1955 and 95 599 kg in 1956. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.1 (citing Harada 2004); Date: 1950–1956
Q10. [paraphrase] At the fishermen's request, the prefecture's Fisheries Division obtained factory documents reporting mercury use; its inspector recommended that the discharge be analysed, but no further survey of the discharge or fishery damage was made. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.1; Date: 1952
Q11. [paraphrase] Local cats that ate large amounts of fish convulsed and died; in 1954 a local newspaper reported a rise in mice in a fishing village after the cats had died out. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.1; Date: 1953–1954
Q12. [paraphrase] A paediatrician at the company hospital examined two young sisters with difficulty walking and speaking and officially notified the public health centre on 1 May 1956; a local countermeasures committee formed on 28 May; 30 cases including 11 deaths were identified by 29 August. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.1 and Table; Date: 1956
Q13. [paraphrase] The first official patients were fishing families living at the end of a small inlet, close enough to the sea to fish from their windows. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.1; Date: 1956
Q14. [paraphrase] The [Regional University] research group reported that the disease was not contagious but food poisoning from fish contaminated by a heavy metal in the bay, and that factory effluent was considered the source of contamination. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.2 (citing [Regional University] 1956); Date: 3 Nov 1956
Q15. [paraphrase] The research group recommended prohibiting fishing or applying the Food Sanitation Act, under which sale of food can be banned and breaches criminally punished. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.2; Date: Feb 1957
Q16. [paraphrase] The prefecture asked the [Health Ministry] whether a total fishing ban under the Act was lawful; the ministry replied that it could not be enforced because there was no clear evidence that all fish in a specified area were contaminated. The fishermen's cooperative instead refrained "voluntarily" from fishing under prefectural guidance. — Source: MOE (2013), ch. on early response; EEA case chapter, Table; Date: Aug 1957
Q17. [paraphrase] A different prefecture applied the Food Sanitation Act itself in another food-poisoning case, while [Prefecture P] did not. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.2; Date: 1957
Q18. [paraphrase] The university group knew nothing of the factory's interior processes and received no help from the factory's engineers; a group member recalled that "Mercury was taken off the list on the assumption that such an expensive material would never be thrown away in the sea". — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.3 (citing Harada 2004, 2005); Date: 1957–1958
Q19. [paraphrase] A British neurologist examined 15 patients in March 1958 and published in *The Lancet* (Sept 1958) that symptoms resembled methylmercury poisoning, naming methylmercury as a possible agent; he was stopped from presenting at the national neurology society by a university professor on the grounds that too many theories would confuse. A US NIH neurologist visiting in Sept 1958 also supported organic mercury. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.3; Date: 1958
Q20. [paraphrase] The chief of the [Health Ministry] Public Health Bureau informed other ministries and local governments that the disease was caused by eating contaminated fish and shellfish and that the factory's discharge had affected the bay; a local newspaper called it the first ministry statement naming the factory. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.3; Date: 7 Jul 1958
Q21. [paraphrase] In September 1958 the factory moved its acetaldehyde drainage outfall from the bay to the river mouth, without residents' knowledge; the company hospital director objected that "if patients were detected in the area around the ... River, it would prove that the discharge was the cause". From 1959 patients with similar symptoms appeared in other villages around the [Inland Sea]. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.3; Date: 1958–1959
Q22. [paraphrase] The university group concluded on 22 July 1959 that the agent was mercury, from clinical features and animal experiments; mercury in bay mud and shellfish fell with distance from the factory. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.3; Date: 1959
Q23. [paraphrase] On 7 October 1959 the company hospital director induced the disease in a cat fed acetaldehyde-process waste water for 78 days; when reported to the factory the result was kept secret and further studies were prohibited. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.3; Date: 1959
Q24. [paraphrase] At an inter-ministerial meeting an [Industry Ministry] representative argued that no similar patients existed near other factories using the same process and that the factory's catalyst mercury was inorganic: "We cannot accept the explanation that waste water from [Company C] contains the etiologic agent". The company publicly refuted the organic-mercury theory; a Tokyo professor claimed bay mercury levels were no higher than elsewhere; the executive director of the national chemical industry association suggested dumped wartime explosives. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.3; Date: Sept–Nov 1959
Q25. [paraphrase] A [Health Ministry] food-poisoning committee recognised organic mercury as the agent on 12 November 1959 without naming the source; a ministry section chief had told it not to conclude that the factory was a cause because this was not "scientifically" proved; the committee was then dissolved without explanation. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.3 and Table; Date: 1959
Q26. [paraphrase] The [Industry Ministry] gave guidance to install effluent treatment; the company completed a coagulation-sedimentation system on 19 December 1959, and most residents believed the discharge would soon stop. Local fishermen had demanded full treatment and suspension of operation; patients held sit-ins at the factory gate demanding compensation. — Source: MOE (2013); EEA case chapter, §2.3; Date: Oct–Dec 1959
Q27. [paraphrase] In December 1959 the company paid patients "sympathy money" under an agreement that they would not demand further compensation even if the disease were found to be caused by the effluent; many, including researchers, took the issue to be settled. — Source: MOE (2013); EEA case chapter, §2.4; Date: 1959
Q28. [paraphrase] A hair survey of 1 645 healthy fishermen around the [Inland Sea] found mercury of 0–920 ppm (median 30 ppm in [Town Z], 21.5 ppm across the sea, versus 2.1 ppm in an unexposed city); the fishermen were not told their results. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.4; Date: 1960
Q29. [paraphrase] A national research council chaired by the president of the national medical association, with all members from Tokyo universities and "primarily sponsored by" [Company C], disputed the organic-mercury theory; the dean of [Regional University] medical school refused to join. Professors in 1960–61 promoted alternative "amine" and "rotten fish" theories, which the mass media took up. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.4; Date: 1960–1961
Q30. [paraphrase] After a new dean took office in April 1961, the [Regional University] medical school took a conciliatory attitude to the company, joined the council and began receiving research funding from the company and the council. — Source: EEA case chapter, §2.4; Date: 1961
