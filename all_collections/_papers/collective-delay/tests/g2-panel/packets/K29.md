# Evidence packet K29
Label: DDT, a persistent organochlorine insecticide used in agriculture, forestry and malaria control
De-identification: not feasible: names kept; coders may know the outcome
t0 (first credible early warning): 1962
Scoring point: t0 + 5 = 1967. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

Note on t0: the EEA case chapter is framed around the 1962 publication of *Silent Spring* as the early warning, so t0 = 1962. Its table also records concerns about insect resistance (1948, 1951) and dairy residues (1948). An EPA historical survey notes scientists' warnings from the mid-1940s. The outcomes file records the alternative.

Sources: "EEA ch. 11" = EEA (2013) *Late lessons from early warnings* II, ch. 11 (Bouwman et al.). "EPA 1975" = US EPA historical survey of the insecticide (July 1975), used only for facts dated up to 1967.

## Evidence
Q1. [paraphrase] The molecule was first synthesised as a laboratory exercise; its insecticidal property was shown only in 1939. — Source: EEA ch. 11, §11.2; EPA 1975; Date: 1873/1874–1939
Q2. [paraphrase] During the Second World War the US produced large quantities to control typhus and malaria abroad. A typhus outbreak in Naples (October 1943) was brought under control by January 1944 after 1.3 million people were treated over three weeks. — Source: EEA ch. 11, §11.2; EPA 1975; Date: 1943–1945
Q3. [paraphrase] After 1945 agricultural and commercial use became widespread in the US. Its popularity rested on low cost, effectiveness, persistence and versatility. — Source: EPA 1975; Date: 1945–1950s
Q4. [paraphrase] US use peaked at about 80 million pounds in 1959. — Source: EPA 1975; Date: 1959
Q5. [paraphrase] It was the primary tool of the first global malaria eradication programme. The walls and ceilings of houses and animal sheds were sprayed so that entire populations were covered. — Source: EEA ch. 11, §11.3.1; Date: 1950s–1960s
Q6. [paraphrase] The 1948 Nobel Prize presentation mentioned flies developing resistance, but treated this as an opportunity for new pesticides rather than an environmental issue. Persistence was then viewed as an advantage, and early usage advice involved indiscriminate dusting of people and food. — Source: EEA ch. 11, §11.2 (citing Nobelprize.org; West & Campbell 1946); Date: 1946–1948
Q7. [paraphrase] Use inside dairies had been prohibited in 1948. In Sweden in the 1950s, flies were reported dying after contact with dairy products containing very high residues. — Source: EEA ch. 11, §11.2 (citing American Journal of Public Health 1949; Löfroth 1971); Date: 1948–1950s
Q8. [paraphrase] Insect resistance was reported from 1951, with earlier signs in Swedish house flies in 1948, and became an obstacle to malaria eradication. — Source: EEA ch. 11, §11.3.1 and Table 11.1; Date: 1948–1951
Q9. [paraphrase] Scientists voiced warnings about hazards linked to the chemical's persistence as early as the mid-1940s. — Source: EPA 1975; Date: mid-1940s
Q10. [paraphrase] The US Forest Service, as a matter of policy, stopped spraying in protective strips around aquatic areas on its lands. In 1958 the Department of Agriculture began phasing out its own use in federal–state control programmes. — Source: EPA 1975; Date: 1957–1958
Q11. [paraphrase] *Silent Spring* described the impacts of chemicals, with special emphasis on this insecticide, on wildlife and human health, opening with an imagined town whose birds had fallen silent. — Source: EEA ch. 11, §11.1 and Table 11.1; Date: 1962
Q12. "the right to make a dollar at whatever cost is seldom challenged" — Source: Carson 1962, *Silent Spring*, quoted in EEA ch. 11, §11.1; Date: 1962
Q13. [paraphrase] Carson warned that cause and effect in the human body are seldom simple and may be widely separated in space and time, and that methods to detect injury before symptoms appear were lacking. — Source: Carson 1962, as quoted in EEA ch. 11, §11.4; Date: 1962
Q14. [paraphrase] The book made the potential environmental problems everyone's concern and stimulated widespread public concern over the chemical's use. — Source: EEA ch. 11, §11.2; EPA 1975; Date: 1962–1963
Q15. [paraphrase] President Kennedy responded to the book by ordering an investigation. The President's Science Advisory Committee issued "Use of Pesticides" (May 1963), and a PSAC panel issued "Restoring the Quality of Our Environment" (November 1965); both weighed the pros and cons of continued use. — Source: EEA ch. 11, §11.1; EPA 1975; Date: 1962–1965
Q16. [paraphrase] Identifying the chemical as an environmental problem depended on advances in analytical chemistry during the 1950s. — Source: EEA ch. 11, §11.2; Date: 1950s
Q17. [paraphrase] Less than a year after the book appeared in Swedish, the Swedish government gave special funding to Stockholm University to set up a laboratory to analyse the chemical in the environment. Its work also identified a second class of industrial contaminant in wildlife (1966). — Source: EEA ch. 11, §11.2 (citing Bernes 1998; Jensen 1966); Date: 1964–1966
Q18. [paraphrase] The first negative effects reported were on birds, a group many people watch. Declines in birds of prey were observed in many parts of the world. — Source: EEA ch. 11, §11.2; Date: late 1950s–1960s
Q19. [paraphrase] Eggshell thinning in British birds of prey was reported and linked in time to the introduction of organochlorine pesticides. — Source: Ratcliffe 1967, *Nature* 215: 208–210 (not opened this session); EEA ch. 11, §11.2 also ties raptor declines to eggshell thinning (citing Bernes 1998); Date: 1967
Q20. [paraphrase] Because the chemical was deliberately released into the environment, its presence there was no great surprise. — Source: EEA ch. 11, §11.2; Date: 1960s
Q21. [paraphrase] Proponents argued that the chemical had a good human health record and that the alternatives were more hazardous to users and more costly. Opponents, while admitting there might be little evidence of direct harm to people, stressed persistence, accumulation in the food chain and proven hazards to fish and wildlife. — Source: EPA 1975 (describing the debate of the 1960s); Date: 1960s
Q22. [paraphrase] Environmental organisations, including the Environmental Defense Fund, the National Audubon Society, the National Wildlife Federation and the Izaak Walton League, began bringing court proceedings over its use at local and federal levels. — Source: EPA 1975; Date: 1967
Q23. [paraphrase] The insecticide remained a key public-health tool where malaria was endemic. South Africa, for example, achieved complete coverage of its malaria areas by indoor spraying in 1958. — Source: EEA ch. 11, Table 11.1; Date: 1958
