# Evidence packet K09

- **Label:** Systemic seed-dressing insecticide "Product G" (active substance "I", of a new insecticide class) made by "Company B", used on sunflower and other field crops in "Country F", and honeybees.
- **De-identification:** Partly done. The product, active substance, manufacturer and country are replaced with neutral labels, and the place names of meetings and demonstrations are removed. Company B is a large multinational agrochemical manufacturer; Country F is a large EU agricultural member state. Coders familiar with the field may still recognise the case.
- **t0:** 1994. This is the first early warning as given by EEA (2013, ch. 16, §16.2.1 "1994: early warnings" and Table 16.1: first clinical signs observed by beekeepers during sunflower nectar flow in the first season of use).
- **Scoring point:** t0 + 5 = 1999. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

## Evidence

Q1. [paraphrase] In Country F, seed-dressing with Product G was authorised in 1991 for sugar beet, in 1992 for maize and in 1993 for sunflower. It was first used on sunflower in 1994. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.2.1 and Table 16.1; Date: 1991–1994.

Q2. [paraphrase] At launch, Product G was marketed as a way to reduce aerial pollution, because it was supposedly confined to the soil and very small amounts were applied to seeds (0.7 mg per sunflower seed, about 56–70 g of active substance per hectare). — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.1; Date: 1993–1994.

Q3. [paraphrase] When Product G was launched, the manufacturer considered that it posed no risk to honeybees provided it was applied as a seed-dressing. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.2.1, citing Company B's 1992 technical information; Date: 1992.

Q4. [paraphrase] In 1993, accepting the manufacturer's claim that honeybees were not exposed to the substance applied as a seed-dressing, the national pesticide toxicity commission issued an assessment favourable to the product. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.4 (Commission for Toxic Products); Date: 1993.

Q5. [paraphrase] After 1994, beekeepers reported alarming clinical signs after several days of foraging on flowering sunflower. Many bees did not return to the hive. Bees clustered in small groups on the ground or hovered disoriented in front of the hive, foraging was abnormal, and queens laid more brood to replace lost foragers. Dead bees were sometimes found in front of hives. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.2.1; Date: 1994 onward.

Q6. [paraphrase] In affected apiaries most hives were hit. Sunflower honey yields fell 40–70% below the previous average, against a normal annual variation of about ±10%. End-of-winter hive losses reached 30–50%, against the usual 5–10%. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.2.1 (based on reports from 20 beekeepers); Date: 1994–1998.

Q7. [paraphrase] Looking for causes, beekeepers noted that Product G had first been used on sunflower in 1994, and that bee problems rose together with the area sown with treated sunflower seed. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.2.1; Date: 1994–1997.

Q8. [paraphrase] The intensity of the signs fluctuated from year to year and place to place, apparently depending on what other food sources were near the hives. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.2.1; Date: 1994–1998.

Q9. [paraphrase] Honeybees forage mostly within 6 km of the hive and up to 12 km, so a colony is exposed to many fields and many potential stressors at once. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, Box 16.2, citing von Frisch 1967 and others; Date: 1967.

Q10. [paraphrase] Poisoning by sprayed pesticides is usually confirmed by many dead and dying bees in front of the hive. Here beekeepers reported mainly the disappearance of foragers. Standard pesticide tests focused on acute mortality, and there were no standardised protocols for chronic or sublethal effects. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.2.1 ("Lethal and sublethal effects"); Date: 1990s.

Q11. [paraphrase] Beekeepers asked Company B for information on the substance's toxicity to honeybees. Company B responded with field and semi-field (tunnel) studies, which according to Company B showed no risk to honeybees. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.2.1 and Table 16.1; Date: 1995–1997.

Q12. [paraphrase] Company B's experiments were presented at an international pest conference in January 1997 and at a meeting of the national agricultural technical coordination body in October 1997. They were criticised, and public scientists were asked to research the issue. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.2.1; Date: 1997.

Q13. [paraphrase] In 1993 the detection limit used by Company B-funded scientists for the substance in plants was 10 ppb. Their studies in this period either could not detect it in pollen and nectar or could not quantify it. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.2.1; Date: 1993–1998.

Q14. [paraphrase] When public research began (1997–1998), the agriculture ministry's food directorate asked for analyses at "the lowest detection limit possible" but "without going below 0.01 mg/kg" (10 ppb). For the 1998 programme it noted that it "is not useful" to work at the lowest detection limits. This limit matched Company B's method, and Company B representatives sat on the committee that drew up the research protocol. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.2.1; Date: 1997–1998.

Q15. [paraphrase] The national technical centre for oilseed crops had already estimated that a detection limit of about 1.4 ppb, much lower than 10 ppb, was needed to find the substance in nectar. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.2.1; Date: 1997–1998.

Q16. [paraphrase] The print media learned of the ministry's detection-limit recommendation and of Company B's role in drafting the protocol. This raised doubts about the ministry's impartiality. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.2.1, citing national daily press 1999; Date: 1999.

Q17. [paraphrase] The first studies by public researchers reported the substance in sunflower leaves and pollen at below 10 ppb, without quantifying it. A 1999 study quantified it in treated sunflower at 3.3 ppb in pollen and 1.9 ppb in nectar. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.2.1, citing Pham-Delègue and Cluzeau 1998 and Stork 1999; Date: 1998–1999.

Q18. "The demonstration, made by [Company B] that [Product G] is not involved, is not made in a rigorous and complete manner. On the other hand, the declarations coming from beekeepers are not rigorous and stable enough for saying that [Product G] is the only cause of honeybee colonies problems." — Source: national pesticide toxicity commission (CTP) 1997, as quoted in EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.4; Date: 1997.

Q19. "The data examined do not allow us to conclude to an unquestionable effect of [the substance] and/or of its metabolites on honeybees and honey production. Conversely, it is also not possible to completely exclude the effect … given the toxic effect at low doses." — Source: CTP 1998, as quoted in EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.4; Date: 1998.

Q20. [paraphrase] Only one member of the commission was a bee specialist. Its honeybee working group was not consulted at this stage. The commission's assessments rested on unstructured expert judgement, with no clear criteria for judging the quality of dossiers submitted by companies. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.4; Date: 1997–1999.

Q21. [paraphrase] At a meeting with representatives of Company B and the agriculture ministry, beekeepers publicly named Product G as the cause of massive bee losses. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, Table 16.1; Date: 1997.

Q22. [paraphrase] A public researcher reported receiving a letter from Company B in January 1998 threatening a lawsuit. Company B's lawyers also wrote to the researcher's superior, seeking to influence the researcher's statements to the press. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.3.1 (personal testimony); Date: 1998.

Q23. [paraphrase] More than 1,000 beekeepers demonstrated in the capital demanding a ban on Product G. In affected apiaries, sunflower honey yields were reported 30–70% below normal. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, Table 16.1; Date: December 1998.

Q24. [paraphrase] A second seed-dressing insecticide (a different active substance), provisionally authorised in December 1995, was also suspected of contributing to the signs. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.2.1 and Table 16.1; Date: 1995–1998.

Q25. [paraphrase] Public scientists (in national research institutes and universities) are civil servants whose salaries are paid by their institutions. Until 1997 the toxicity studies came from Company B or Company B-funded scientists. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, §16.2.1 and note 4; Date: 1994–1998.

Q26. [paraphrase] Pesticide authorisation operated under the EU-wide plant-protection directive adopted in 1991, with national marketing authorisation advised by the national toxicity commission. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 16, Table 16.1 and §16.4; Date: 1991.
