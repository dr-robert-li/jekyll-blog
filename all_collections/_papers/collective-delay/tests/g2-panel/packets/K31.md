# Evidence packet K31
Label: Invasive alien species: animals, plants and micro-organisms moved by human trade and travel that spread and damage ecosystems
De-identification: not feasible: the hazard is a general phenomenon whose name is its description; coders may know the outcome
t0 (first credible early warning): 1958
Scoring point: t0 + 5 = 1963. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

Note on t0: the EEA table begins with Pliny the Elder (77 AD) and Darwin (1830s). The EEA chapter identifies Elton's 1958 monograph as the starting point of the scientific understanding of the threat, so t0 = 1958.

Sources: "EEA ch. 20" = EEA (2013) *Late lessons from early warnings* II, ch. 20 (Brunel et al.). "IPPC" = International Plant Protection Convention secretariat, "History of the IPPC" (ippc.int). "Elton 1958" = C. S. Elton, *The Ecology of Invasions by Animals and Plants*. The book was not opened this session; items marked [Elton, not opened] paraphrase its widely cited examples and should be weighted accordingly.

## Evidence
Q1. [paraphrase] Pliny the Elder wrote that an invasion of rabbits in the Balearic Islands was a very severe problem requiring effective control. — Source: EEA ch. 20, §20.3 (citing Scalera & Zaghi 2004); Date: 77 AD
Q2. [paraphrase] Darwin noted the invasive behaviour of some alien species during the Beagle voyage, and his reflections fed into his theory of natural selection. — Source: EEA ch. 20, §20.3 and Table 20.1; Date: 1830s–1859
Q3. "rapidly displace the aborigines and replace them" — Source: J. D. Hooker 1864, on introduced plants, quoted in EEA ch. 20, §20.3; Date: 1864
Q4. [paraphrase] Concerns about the impact of alien species on native vegetation, particularly on islands, began to be voiced; most alien species, however, were regarded as curiosities rather than a significant threat. — Source: EEA ch. 20, §20.3; Date: 19th century
Q5. [paraphrase] A North American aphid accidentally introduced into Europe around 1865 devastated much of Europe's grape-growing regions. In 1881 five countries signed an agreement to control its spread, the start of international plant protection. — Source: IPPC; Date: 1865–1881
Q6. [paraphrase] An International Convention for the Protection of Plants was signed in Rome. — Source: IPPC; Date: 1929
Q7. [paraphrase] The FAO adopted the International Plant Protection Convention, which entered into force in April 1952 and superseded earlier plant-protection agreements. It covers pests of plants, not invasive species in general. — Source: IPPC; Date: 1951–1952
Q8. [paraphrase] The United Kingdom had a statute, the Destructive Imported Animals Act 1932, directed at specific imported animals. — Source: legislation.gov.uk (title and existence confirmed; text not read); Date: 1932
Q9. [paraphrase] In a European tradition dating from the 19th and early 20th centuries, floristic studies of "adventive" species already considered humans as agents of biotic invasions. — Source: EEA ch. 20, §20.3 (citing Kueffer & Hirsch Hadorn 2008); Date: 19th–early 20th century
Q10. [paraphrase] Elton's monograph set out his concern about the escalating impact of invasive alien species on natural ecosystems and the need to conserve species diversity from it. It is widely seen as the founding text of invasion biology. — Source: EEA ch. 20, §20.3 and Table 20.1; Date: 1958
Q11. [paraphrase] Elton argued that the worldwide mingling of species through human transport was causing large dislocations in nature. His examples included a fungal blight that destroyed American chestnut forests, the spread of an introduced rodent (the muskrat) across central Europe after a release in Bohemia, the sea lamprey's invasion of the Great Lakes and rabbits in Australia. — Source: Elton 1958 [Elton, not opened]; Date: 1958
Q12. [paraphrase] The routes Elton described were both deliberate introductions (game, fur animals, ornamental plants, biological control agents) and accidental ones via shipping and trade in goods and plants. — Source: Elton 1958 [Elton, not opened]; Date: 1958
Q13. [paraphrase] In Europe these ideas were slow to spread. For many biologists and conservationists, biological invasions were still happening "somewhere else", such as rabbits in Australia or water hyacinth in African lakes and waterways. — Source: EEA ch. 20, §20.3 (describing mid-20th-century perceptions); Date: 1950s–1960s
Q14. [paraphrase] The sea lamprey was first recorded in Lake Ontario in 1835. Improvements to a shipping canal bypassing Niagara Falls in the late 1800s and early 1900s let it reach the other Great Lakes: Erie by 1921, Michigan and Huron by 1936–1937, Superior by 1938. — Source: Great Lakes Fishery Commission, "Sea lamprey" (glfc.org); Date: 1835–1938
Q15. [paraphrase] Before the invasion, Canada and the US harvested about 15 million pounds of lake trout a year in the upper Great Lakes. Lamprey populations exploded by the late 1940s, and by the early 1960s the catch was about 300 000 pounds, roughly 2% of the earlier average, devastating the fishery and related jobs. — Source: Great Lakes Fishery Commission, "Sea lamprey"; Date: late 1940s–early 1960s
Q16. [paraphrase] A binational Canada–US commission was established in 1955 to address the Great Lakes fishery, including control of the sea lamprey. It was a single-species, single-region body. — Source: Great Lakes Fishery Commission, "Sea lamprey"; Date: 1955
