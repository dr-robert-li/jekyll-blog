# Evidence packet K16

**Label:** Engineered materials with structure at the scale of 1–100 nanometres, and ultrafine particles.
**De-identification:** Partly feasible: no product or firm is singled out. The technology field is named, so coders may know later developments. Score only from the items below.
**t0:** 1992. EEA's chronology gives this as its first toxicological finding: surface area is a better descriptor than mass for the adverse effects of titanium dioxide particles in rats. EEA also lists a 1986 speculative concern (Q6).
**Scoring point:** t0 + 5 = 1997. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

## Evidence

Q1. [paraphrase] The term "nanotechnology" is first used, referring to the ability to engineer materials precisely at the nanometre level. — Source: Taniguchi 1974, as described in EEA 2013, ch. 22 §22.1 and Table 22.2; Date: 1974

Q2. [paraphrase] The scanning tunnelling microscope is developed, followed by the atomic force microscope, which allows nanostructures to be visualised. — Source: Binnig et al. 1982, 1986, cited in EEA 2013, ch. 22 §22.2 and Table 22.2; Date: 1981–1986

Q3. [paraphrase] Fullerenes are discovered at a US university (Rice). — Source: Kroto et al. 1985, cited in EEA 2013, ch. 22 §22.2 and Table 22.2; Date: 1985

Q4. [paraphrase] Carbon nanotubes are discovered by a researcher at an electronics company's laboratory. — Source: Iijima 1991, Nature 354:56, cited in EEA 2013, ch. 22 §22.2 and Table 22.2; Date: 1991

Q5. [paraphrase] Early development is driven by advances in materials science and by scientific breakthroughs (fullerenes, quantum dots, nanotubes), together with new visualisation instruments. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 22 §22.2; Date: 1981–1991

Q6. [paraphrase] A popular book raises concern about potential risks of nanotechnology. These are concerns about self-replicating molecular machines, not toxicological data. — Source: Drexler, *Engines of Creation* (1986), cited in EEA 2013, ch. 22 §22.4 and Table 22.2; Date: 1986

Q7. [paraphrase] Rat studies with titanium dioxide find that particle surface area describes adverse pulmonary effects better than mass does. Ultrafine (nanoscale) particles of an otherwise low-toxicity material produce greater inflammatory effects than larger particles at the same mass. — Source: Oberdörster et al. 1992, cited in EEA 2013, ch. 22 §22.4 and Table 22.2; Date: 1992

Q8. [paraphrase] The 1992 finding comes from inhalation-toxicology research on particles (the authors were at a US university, the University of Rochester), not from a nanotechnology programme. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 22 §22.4 (the research on ultrafine particles of the 1990s "now feeds into what we know about the potential risk of nanomaterials"); Date: 1992

Q9. [paraphrase] One study finds a statistically significant increase in malignant lung tumours in rats after chronic inhalation of nano-sized titanium dioxide. — Source: Heinrich et al. 1995, Inhal. Toxicol. 7:533, cited in EEA 2013, ch. 22 §22.4; Date: 1995

Q10. [paraphrase] Epidemiological research on ambient fine particles links them to increased cardiopulmonary mortality in US cities. — Source: Dockery et al. 1993, NEJM 329:1753 (Harvard Six Cities Study) (contemporary; text not opened this session); Date: 1993

Q11. [paraphrase] A hypothesis is published that ultrafine particles in urban air, through their large number and surface area, provoke lung inflammation and cardiovascular effects. — Source: Seaton et al. 1995, Lancet 345:176 (contemporary; text not opened this session); Date: 1995

Q12. [paraphrase] Ultrafine particles are defined as airborne particles smaller than 0.1 micron (under 100 nm). They come from many sources, including diesel exhaust and incinerator stacks. Since engineered nanomaterials fall in the same size range, the ultrafine literature is the nearest body of toxicological evidence. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 22 §22.4 (definitions); Date: 1992–1997

Q13. [paraphrase] Historical analogies are available with fibre toxicity, where particle dimension determines pathogenicity, as for asbestos. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 22 §22.4 (analogies with ultrafine particles and asbestos); Date: pre-1992 knowledge base

Q14. [paraphrase] Engineered nanomaterials need not differ in chemical identity from bulk forms of the same substance. Chemical-control law of the time (e.g. US TSCA, 1976) distinguishes chemicals by molecular structure, not by particle size. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 22 §22.5 (describing TSCA's design, which dates from 1976); Date: 1976

Q15. [paraphrase] Most activity in the field is research and development. Public and private attention is on R&D rather than on products on the market. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 22 §22.3 (describing the emerging stage of the technology); Date: 1990s

Q16. [paraphrase] Government funding of academic research is a main driver of nanoscience publication and knowledge creation, with a lag before knowledge diffuses into applications. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 22 §22.2 (Linkov et al.; Igami & Okazaki); Date: 1990s

Q17. [paraphrase] Most intra-tracheal and intra-peritoneal toxicity methods bypass the defences of the upper respiratory tract. Historically this has led to questions about the biological relevance of such studies. — Source: Oiser et al. 1997, cited in EEA 2013, ch. 22 §22.4; Date: 1997

Q18. [paraphrase] Particle and fibre toxicology already has a body of methods and precedents from occupational dust diseases and ambient air pollution, which inhalation toxicologists apply to ultrafine particles. — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 22 §22.4; Date: 1992–1997

Q19. [paraphrase] There is no agreed definition of a "nanomaterial" or of "nanotechnology". The term is "framed and reframed by various actors". — Source: EEA 2013, ch. 22 §22.1; Date: 1974–1997
