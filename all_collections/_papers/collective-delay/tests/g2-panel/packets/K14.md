# Evidence packet K14

- **Label:** Hormone H, a synthetic oestrogen (with related natural and synthetic sex steroids) used as a growth promoter in cattle, sheep and poultry, given as implants or in feed.
- **De-identification:** Partial. The compound is not named, but the human-health evidence (a rare cancer in daughters of women treated with the compound as a drug in pregnancy) points clearly to it. Coders may identify it and may know the outcome. Score only from the items below.
- **t0:** 1971. EEA dates the first early warning to the early 1970s, when the compound was confirmed as a human carcinogen. The key publication is in 1971.
- **Scoring point:** t0 + 5 = 1976. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

## Evidence

Q1. [paraphrase] Oestrogens are biologically active at very low levels (about 0.1–1 pg/ml of serum). It had been known since 1940 that they affect development of the male reproductive system in mammals. — Source: EEA 2001 *Late lessons I* ch. 14 §14.1 (citing Zuckerman 1940); Date: 1940

Q2. [paraphrase] After the Second World War the growth-promoting effects of oestrogens, alone or with androgens, were recognised and used to raise meat output. Hormone H, "a cheap, better absorbed analogue" of the natural hormone oestradiol-17β, became the favoured growth promoter for cattle, sheep and poultry in many countries. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 14 §14.1; Date: 1945–1970

Q3. [paraphrase] Several combined hormonal implants were approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as growth promoters: progesterone/oestradiol benzoate (1956), testosterone propionate/oestradiol benzoate (1958) and zeranol (1969). Alternatives to Hormone H were therefore on the market. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 14 Table 14.1 (Henderson et al. 1988); Date: 1956–1969

Q4. [paraphrase] A Boston case-control study found that seven of eight young women with vaginal clear-cell adenocarcinoma, an extremely rare cancer at their age, had mothers who took Hormone H during pregnancy. — Source: Herbst, Ulfelder and Poskanzer, *N Engl J Med* 284:878–881; Date: April 1971

Q5. [paraphrase] The harm appeared 15–20 years after prenatal exposure, in the next generation, and was traced to the drug only because the cancer was so rare at those ages. — Source: Herbst et al. 1971; EEA 2001 ch. 8 (the same compound as a pregnancy drug); Date: 1971

Q6. [paraphrase] Concerns about Hormone H as a growth promoter were raised in the early 1970s when it was confirmed to be a human carcinogen. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 14 §14.1 and Table 14.2; Date: early 1970s

Q7. [paraphrase] The scientific consensus was that the risk to meat consumers was insignificant, because residues in meat were very low (below the limit of analytical detection) compared with medical doses. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 14 §14.1; Date: early 1970s

Q8. [paraphrase] US food law already contained the 1958 Delaney Clause, which "prohibits food for human consumption that contains carcinogenic substances". It was hard to apply because most foods contain trace carcinogens, and regulators relied on "minimum detectable levels". — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 14 §14.1; Date: 1958 (clause); early 1970s (practice)

Q9. [paraphrase] The case for using Hormone H as a growth promoter was purely economic, with no benefit to the treated animals or the environment. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 14 §14.4; Date: assessment of the 1950s–1970s use

Q10. [paraphrase] The FDA estimated that removing Hormone H would cost consumers about USD 500 million a year. The estimate rested on a number of questionable assumptions. The estimated consumer health risk was put at about one cancer in 133 years. — Source: Jakes, *Preventive Medicine* 5:438–453, as summarised in EEA 2001 ch. 14 §14.1; Date: 1976

Q11. [paraphrase] The farming lobby claimed that restricting Hormone H would have serious economic consequences, although alternative growth promoters were already approved. The pharmaceutical industry was developing more hormonal growth promoters. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 14 §14.1; Date: mid-1970s

Q12. [paraphrase] Defenders of residue safety pointed to the safety of oestrogens in the oral contraceptive pill and to the high natural oestrogen levels of pregnant women. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 14 §14.1; Date: early-to-mid 1970s

Q13. [paraphrase] Prepubertal children have very low natural oestrogen levels, close to the limits of detection, so any extra oestrogen is a relatively large share of their total. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 14 §14.3; Date: physiological point available in the 1970s

Q14. [paraphrase] In the early 1970s the hormone could not be measured reliably at residue levels, and metabolites with possible oestrogenic activity were not assayed. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 14 §14.3; Date: 1970s

Q15. [paraphrase] Hormone H stayed in use as a growth promoter in some European countries at this time. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 14 §14.1; Date: 1970s

Q16. [paraphrase] A 1970 report suggested that the insecticide DDT lowered oestradiol in birds through an oestrogen-like action, an early sign of oestrogenic contaminants acting on wildlife. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 14 §14.2 (citing Peakall 1970); Date: 1970

Q17. [paraphrase] Growth reduction was observed in catfish exposed to Hormone H, suggesting effects across many species. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 14 §14.2 and Table 14.2 (Peakall/Bulkley 1972); Date: 1972

Q18. [paraphrase] Feminisation of male birds (both ovarian and testicular tissue) was induced by a synthetic oestrogen. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 14 §14.2 (citing DeLong et al. 1973); Date: 1973

Q19. [paraphrase] Drug agencies paid little attention to environmental effects of excreted veterinary hormones. It was widely assumed that excreted drugs would be very dilute and degrade quickly. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 14 §14.2; Date: 1970s assumption

Q20. [paraphrase] Growth promoters were introduced to improve production efficiency. Human health, possible environmental effects and animal welfare were not given significant attention when they were adopted. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 14 §14.3; Date: 1950s–1970s

Q21. [paraphrase] Through pregnancy use from the 1940s, the same compound had already been given in high doses to several million pregnant women as a drug, so a large exposed population and a medical precedent existed. — Source: EEA 2001 ch. 8 (the compound as a pregnancy drug); Date: 1940s–1971
