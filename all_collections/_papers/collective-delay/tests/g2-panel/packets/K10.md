# Evidence packet K10

- **Label:** Asbestos dust in the manufacturing of asbestos textiles and products.
- **De-identification:** Not feasible; the mineral and the named early observers are the evidence. Names are kept. Coders may know the outcome; score only from the items below.
- **t0:** 1898. This is the first credible early warning as given by EEA (2001, ch. 5, §5.2 and Table 5.1: the report of UK Factory Inspector Lucy Deane). EEA Summary points call it the "first clear and credible early warning".
- **Scoring point:** t0 + 5 = 1903. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

## Evidence

Q1. [paraphrase] Mining of chrysotile ("white") asbestos began in Thetford, Canada, in 1879. Within 20 years more than 100 products had been developed from the "magic mineral". — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 5, §§5.1–5.2; Date: 1879–1898.

Q2. [paraphrase] The Turner Brothers asbestos factory had operated in Rochdale since 1880. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 5, §5.2; Date: 1880.

Q3. [paraphrase] The Cape Asbestos Company was formed in London in 1893 to mine and process South African blue asbestos (crocidolite). — Source: company registration history as summarised in Castleman 1996 (cited by EEA 2001, ch. 5); Date: 1893.

Q4. [paraphrase] The asbestos manufacturing workforce included women working in the carding, spinning and weaving rooms of textile works. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 5, §5.2; Date: 1890s–1900s.

Q5. "On account of their easily demonstrated danger to the health of workers and because of ascertained cases of injury to bronchial tubes and lungs medically attributed to the employment of the sufferer." These are Deane's reasons for including asbestos work among the four dusty occupations under observation that year. — Source: Deane 1898, Annual Report of HM Chief Inspector of Factories, as quoted in EEA 2001, ch. 5, §5.2; Date: 1898.

Q6. "The evil effects of asbestos dust have also instigated a microscopic examination of the mineral dust by HM Medical Inspector. Clearly revealed was the sharp glass-like jagged nature of the particles, and where they are allowed to rise and to remain suspended in the air of the room in any quantity, the effects have been found to be injurious as might have been expected." — Source: Deane 1898, as quoted in EEA 2001, ch. 5, §5.2; Date: 1898.

Q7. [paraphrase] The women inspectors' observations appeared in the annual reports of HM Chief Inspector of Factories, which were widely circulated among policymakers and politicians. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 5, §5.2; Date: 1898.

Q8. [paraphrase] The women inspectors were lay observers, not medical scientists, so their reports might not have been classed as "expert opinion". — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 5, §5.2; Date: 1898.

Q9. [paraphrase] Deane proposed the kinds of mortality statistics that would help establish the extent of harm. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 5, §5.2; Date: c. 1898.

Q10. [paraphrase] Women Inspectors of Factories were first appointed in the UK in 1893. — Source: Home Office factory inspectorate history; EEA 2001, ch. 5, §5.2 (Deane as "one of the first Women Inspectors of Factories"); Date: 1893.

Q11. [paraphrase] The UK factory inspectorate gained its first Medical Inspector of Factories (Thomas Legge) in 1898. A microscopic examination of asbestos dust was carried out by HM Medical Inspector (Q6). — Source: factory inspectorate history; EEA 2001, ch. 5, §5.2; Date: 1898.

Q12. [paraphrase] In 1899 a London hospital physician (Dr Montague Murray, Charing Cross Hospital) saw a 33-year-old man with lung disease attributed to inhaled asbestos dust. He had worked about 14 years in an asbestos textile works, the first ten in the carding room, which he called the most risky part of the work. The patient said that of the ten people in that room when he started, he was the only survivor, and the others had died at about thirty. This was known to the treating physician; the co-workers' deaths were not investigated. — Source: Murray's account, as quoted in EEA 2001, ch. 5, §5.2; Date: 1899 (clinical observation).

Q13. [paraphrase] The disease seen (lung fibrosis) followed years of exposure. Symptoms appeared long after work began (Q12). — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 5, §5.2 (Q12 case); Date: 1899.

Q14. [paraphrase] Lung disease from dusty trades was already well known: Thackrah described the effects of occupational dusts on health, including grinders' lung disease, in 1831. — Source: Thackrah 1831, *The Effects of Arts, Trades and Professions on Health and Longevity*; Date: 1831.

Q15. [paraphrase] UK factory law already gave inspectors power to require fans or other mechanical means to remove dust generated and inhaled to an injurious extent in factories. — Source: Factory and Workshop Act 1878 (41 & 42 Vict. c. 16), s. 36; Date: 1878.

Q16. [paraphrase] The Workmen's Compensation Act 1897 provided compensation for personal injury by accident in specified employments. It did not cover industrial diseases such as dust-induced lung disease. — Source: Workmen's Compensation Act 1897 (60 & 61 Vict. c. 37); Date: 1897.

Q17. [paraphrase] The early evidence of harm came from government factory inspectors and a hospital physician, not from the asbestos industry. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 5, §5.2; Date: 1898–1899.

Q18. [paraphrase] Asbestos was valued for heat resistance and fire-proofing, notably for lagging boilers, steam pipes and engines. The rapid growth in products (Q1) reflected wide industrial demand. — Source: EEA 2001, ch. 5, §§5.1–5.2; Date: 1879–1903.
