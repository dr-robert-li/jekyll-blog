# Evidence packet K26
Label: Hormone X, a synthetic steroid used as the active ingredient of an oral medication taken daily by millions of women
De-identification: partly de-identified (substance and manufacturer names replaced; river and agency names kept); coders may recognise the case
t0 (first credible early warning): 1978
Scoring point: t0 + 5 = 1983. Every item below is dated at or before this year.

Names replaced: [Hormone X] = the synthetic steroid; [the medication] = the oral product containing it; [Firm S] = the pharmaceutical company that developed it; [Drug D] = a different synthetic oestrogen given in pregnancy; [Agent T] = a marine antifouling compound. Source "EEA case chapter" = EEA (2013) *Late lessons from early warnings* II, case chapter on [Hormone X]. Where the EEA text gives no exact year, the date given is the period the chapter places the event in.

## Evidence
Q1. [paraphrase] [Hormone X], the first orally active synthetic steroidal oestrogen, was synthesised in Berlin by chemists at [Firm S], at a time of intense research into sex hormones for fertility control. — Source: EEA case chapter, §13.1.1 (citing Inhoffen & Hohlweg 1938); Date: 1938
Q2. [paraphrase] [Firm S] first marketed [Hormone X] for menopausal symptoms; it was readily absorbed orally and very resistant to degradation and metabolism in the gut. These properties paved the way for [the medication]. — Source: EEA case chapter, §13.1.1; Date: 1943
Q3. [paraphrase] In the same period a medical researcher reported potent oestrogenic properties in several synthetic compounds, including [Drug D] and two industrial chemicals later used in plastics and detergents. — Source: EEA case chapter, §13.1.1 (citing Dodds et al. 1936, 1938); Date: 1936–1938
Q4. [paraphrase] [The medication] was approved in the United States in early 1961; on 4 December 1961 the UK Minister of Health announced it could be prescribed through the National Health Service at a subsidised price. — Source: EEA case chapter, §13.1.2 (citing Time 1961); Date: 1961
Q5. [paraphrase] UK users rose from about 50 000 in 1962 to about one million in 1969, out of an estimated 10 million users worldwide; it appeared on the cover of a major US news magazine in April 1967. — Source: EEA case chapter, §13.1.2 and Table 13.1; Date: 1962–1969
Q6. [paraphrase] [The medication] gave women unprecedented control over fertility and was linked from the start with hopes of curbing population growth; it is credited with a major role in the women's movement. — Source: EEA case chapter, §13.1.1–13.1.2 (describing 1960s reception); Date: 1960s
Q7. [paraphrase] Some scientists began speculating that [the medication] might cause environmental problems: oral drugs are inefficient, most of the dose passes through the body into wastewater, and treatment plants were not designed to remove pharmaceuticals. — Source: EEA case chapter, §13.1.2 (citing Tabak & Bunch 1970); Date: 1970
Q8. [paraphrase] A report described steroidal oestrogens in wastewater, river water and potable water. — Source: EEA case chapter, §13.2.1 (citing Tabak et al. 1981); Date: 1981
Q9. [paraphrase] A study reported low sperm counts in 93% of men about to undergo vasectomy. — Source: EEA case chapter, Table 13.1 (Kinloch Nelson & Bunge); Date: 1979 (EEA table date)
Q10. [paraphrase] During a routine health check of a small sample of fish from the River Lea, a water-authority biologist found eggs developing in the testes of 5 of 26 male roach, a commonly caught angling species. — Source: EEA case chapter, §13.2.1; Date: 1978
Q11. [paraphrase] A published survey put the normal incidence of hermaphroditism in the same species at about 1 in 1 000, more than 100 times lower than in the River Lea fish. — Source: EEA case chapter, §13.2.1 (citing Jafri & Ensor 1979); Date: 1979
Q12. [paraphrase] Larger samples confirmed the observation. Incidence rose with age, reaching 20% in fish older than six years, which the water authority's senior scientist read as a "cumulative effect with time". — Source: EEA case chapter, §13.2.1 (citing Sweeting 1981); Date: 1981
Q13. "some risk of endocrine disturbance in human consumers of the water could be implied" — Source: Sweeting 1981, quoted in EEA case chapter, §13.2.1; Date: 1981
Q14. [paraphrase] The river received discharges from numerous sewage treatment works and was also a major drinking-water source for North London: water was abstracted, used, treated and returned to the river several times downstream. — Source: EEA case chapter, §13.2.1; Date: c. 1981
Q15. [paraphrase] The water authority, then government-owned, was proactive in researching the finding further because of its possible relevance to public health. — Source: EEA case chapter, §13.2.1; Date: c. 1979–1982
Q16. [paraphrase] Under a research contract from the water authority to a university, female rats given river water from the drinking-water abstraction point for 12 months showed persistent oestrus. Water taken part-way through drinking-water treatment had no effect. The studies were never published. — Source: EEA case chapter, §13.2.1; Date: c. 1979–1982
Q17. [paraphrase] Autopsies of water voles and wild rats trapped around the sewage works showed no obvious gross reproductive abnormalities. — Source: EEA case chapter, §13.2.1; Date: c. 1979–1982
Q18. [paraphrase] The water authority presented the rat results to a standing committee of the Department of Health. After further studies, the committee agreed there was enough information to discount any risk to human consumers of water abstracted from the lower river. — Source: EEA case chapter, §13.2.1; Date: early 1980s (EEA gives no exact year)
Q19. [paraphrase] None of the scientific reports circulated between government and the water industry formally stated that [Hormone X] might cause intersex in fish, and none linked the finding to the reports of steroidal oestrogens in wastewater, river and potable water. — Source: EEA case chapter, §13.2.1; Date: c. 1978–1983
Q20. [paraphrase] Routine health checks of male roach in a UK river showed oocytes in testes (intersex) at a very high rate compared with the norm. — Source: EEA case chapter, Table 13.1; Date: 1982
Q21. [paraphrase] A different synthetic oestrogen, [Drug D], given to pregnant women, had been linked to a rare vaginal cancer in their daughters, showing that prenatal exposure to synthetic oestrogens could cause harm decades later. — Source: EEA (2001) *Late lessons* I, chapter on [Drug D]; Date: 1971
Q22. [paraphrase] A marine antifouling compound, [Agent T], was found to cause masculinisation (imposex) in female sea snails from the early 1970s and was linked to the failure of commercial oyster stocks in a French bay. Its effects occurred at very low concentrations. — Source: EEA (2013) ch. 12 §12.1.2; EEA (2001) ch. 13; Date: early 1970s–1981
