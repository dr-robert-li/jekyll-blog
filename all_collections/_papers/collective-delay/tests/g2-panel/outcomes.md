## K01 — DBCP (dibromochloropropane) pesticide, male infertility (EEA Vol II ch 9)

- First meaningful regulatory action: US OSHA Emergency Temporary Standard limiting worker DBCP exposure, 9 Sep 1977 (permanent 1 ppb standard published 11 Mar 1978); in the same month US EPA announced suspension of DBCP distribution under FIFRA (made permanent Nov 1979, except Hawaiian pineapple; that use banned 1985) [admit: EEA 2013 ch. 9 §9.4, §9.6]
- Lag (years): 19 [admit: EEA 2013 ch. 9]
- Locus: unresolved between downstream (OSHA workplace exposure limit) and root (EPA suspension of distribution), both September 1977 [abstain: unverifiable — EEA gives the OSHA date (9 Sep) but only the month for the EPA suspension; order within the month not confirmed]
- Notes: 1961 FDA registration with a *recommended* 1 ppm limit is excluded (non-binding). The chapter's own section heading dates early warnings from 1961 (published animal data); t0 = 1961 would give lag 16. The 1958 studies were industry studies (Shell, Dow).

- t0: 1993 [admit: EEA 2013 ch. 10 §10.3 (risks "re-discovered" by accident: Krishnan et al. 1993 found BPA leaching from polycarbonate)]. The chapter has no early-warnings table. The oestrogenicity finding of 1936 (§10.1) predates the polycarbonate use (1957) and is not used as t0.
- First meaningful regulatory action: EU lowering of the BPA specific migration limit in food-contact plastics from 3 to 0.6 mg/kg (Commission Directive 2004/19/EC, 2004) [abstain: unverifiable — directive text not opened; the 0.6 mg/kg SML is confirmed as later carried into Reg. 10/2011 (Commission Regulation (EU) 2018/213 recital)]
- Lag (years): 11 (1993 → 2004) [abstain: unverifiable, as above]. Alternative: the EU ban on BPA in infant feeding bottles, effective 2011, gives a lag of 18 [admit: EEA 2013 ch. 10]. National baby-bottle bans in Canada and France (2010) would give 17 [abstain: unverifiable].
- Locus: the 2004 measure is a migration limit (downstream, an exposure limit). The 2010/2011 baby-bottle bans are use bans in one product category (root, partial). The P13 coding depends on which is "first meaningful".
- Notes: the 1990 SML of 3 mg/kg (Directive 90/128/EEC) predates t0 and is a general listing, not a response [admit: EEA 2013 ch. 10 §10.2]. Later steps were an SML of 0.05 mg/kg (2018) and a ban in most food-contact materials (adopted 19 Dec 2024, in force 20 Jan 2025) [abstain: non-authoritative source — trade and legal summaries]. Early research was academic (Stanford, Krishnan et al.; Granada, Brotons et al.); the affiliation of Biles et al. 1997 is not confirmed [abstain: unverifiable]; industry-sponsored GLP studies came after 1998 (Tyl et al. 2002).

## K26 — Ethinyl oestradiol (EE2, contraceptive-pill hormone) in the aquatic environment (EEA Vol II ch 13)

EE2 was then placed on the first WFD Watch List (2015), which imposes monitoring, not limits; this is excluded as non-restrictive [abstain: non-authoritative source — search-result summary of JRC report; Decision 2015/495 not opened]. ## K27 — PCBs (polychlorinated biphenyls) (EEA Vol I ch 6)
- t0: 1937 [admit: EEA 2001 ch. 6 Table 6.1, "1937 Chloracne and liver damage observed in experiments with rats ... both labour regulators and manufacturers were made aware"; §6.1 (Drinker meeting)]. This departs from the EEA synthesis chapter (ch. 16), which states "the first cases of PCB-induced chloracne were documented in 1899". EEA ch. 6 itself attributes the 1899 chloracne to "the chlorinated organic industry", and commercial PCB production began only in 1929 [refute: EEA 2001 ch. 6 §6.1 — the 1899 cases predate PCB manufacture; correction: earliest PCB-specific warning is 1936–37]. Alternatives: 1899 (EEA ch. 16) and 1966 (Jensen, "first warning that PCBs were becoming ubiquitous in the environment", EEA ch. 6).
- First meaningful regulatory action: Sweden banned PCBs for "open" uses (sealants, paints, plastics) in 1972 [admit: EEA 2001 ch. 6 §6.3 and Table 6.1]. Monsanto's 1971 voluntary restriction of Aroclor chlorination is excluded as voluntary. Later actions: OECD Council Decision C(73)1, Feb 1973; US TSCA §6(e), 1976 [admit: EEA 2001 ch. 6 §6.3].
- Lag (years): 35 (from 1937) [admit: EEA 2001 ch. 6]; 73 if t0 = 1899; 6 if t0 = 1966
- Locus: root — a ban on uses of the substance [admit: EEA 2001 ch. 6 §6.3]
- Notes: the 1937 research was commissioned by an industrial user (Halowax) and the follow-up by the producer (Monsanto), so it was industry-funded. The 1966 environmental warning came from publicly funded Swedish research (Stockholm University laboratory, EEA 2013 ch. 11). The packet uses facts from a 2023 Vermont civil complaint (non-authoritative for outcomes; used only for 1935–39 facts). Because t0 has three defensible values, run sensitivity on P1 with this case excluded.

## K28 — Sulphur dioxide and coal smoke: London smog (and later acidification) (EEA Vol I ch 10)
- t0: 1952 [admit: EEA 2001 ch. 10 Table 10.1, first entry "1952 A dense smog kills more than 2 000 people in London"]. The chapter's second hazard, transboundary acidification, has t0 = 1968 (Odén) [admit: EEA 2001 ch. 10 Table 10.1].
- First meaningful regulatory action: UK Clean Air Act 1956, which banned emissions of dark smoke and allowed smoke control areas requiring smokeless fuels [admit: Met Office, "The Great Smog of 1952"; EEA 2001 ch. 10 ("the subsequent Clean Air Act ... smoke-free areas")]
- Lag (years): 4 [admit: Met Office; EEA 2001 ch. 10]
- Locus: root for smoke (it restricts what may be burned and emitted at source). For SO2 specifically it is downstream: EEA says the Act "considered the smoke component", and the main method of cutting ground-level SO2 under the 1958 Alkali Act revision was tall chimneys, i.e. dilution [admit: EEA 2001 ch. 10]. Coded root, with the SO2 caveat [abstain: tier-floor: unearned confidence — locus depends on whether the hazard is defined as smoke or SO2].
- Notes: for the acidification hazard (t0 = 1968), the first binding instrument with emission cuts is the 1985 CLRTAP sulphur protocol (30% reduction): lag 17, root. The 1979 CLRTAP convention is framework only [admit: EEA 2001 ch. 10 Table 10.1]. Early health evidence came from public bodies: the Ministry of Health, the London County Council and St Bartholomew's Hospital.

## K29 — DDT (EEA Vol II ch 11)

## K31 — Invasive alien species (EEA Vol II ch 20)
- t0: 1958 (Elton, *The Ecology of Invasions by Animals and Plants*) [admit: EEA 2013 ch. 20 §20.3 and Table 20.1, "seen by many as a starting point"]. The EEA table's earlier entries (Pliny 77 AD, Darwin 1830s) are not credible early warnings of a modern hazard.
- First meaningful regulatory action: the earliest general (non-sectoral) binding national measure found is the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, s.14, which makes it an offence to release, or allow to escape, non-native animals or listed plants into the wild [admit: legislation.gov.uk, ukpga/1981/69/section/14/enacted]. That no earlier general post-1958 national measure exists elsewhere is unconfirmed [abstain: unverifiable]. The first global binding instrument is CBD Art. 8(h), in force end of 1993 [admit: EEA 2013 ch. 20 §20.2–20.3].
- Lag (years): 23 (UK 1981) [admit for the date; abstain: unverifiable for "first"]; 35 if CBD 1993 is used
- Locus: root — prohibits introduction or release, i.e. acts on the pathway at entry [admit: legislation.gov.uk]
- Notes: binding sectoral measures predate t0: the 1881 phylloxera agreement, the 1929 Rome plant-protection convention, the IPPC (1951, in force 1952) [admit: ippc.int, History of the IPPC], the UK Destructive Imported Animals Act 1932 [admit: legislation.gov.uk, title only] and the 1955 Great Lakes Fishery Commission [admit: glfc.org].
