### ***TL;DR***

* **Software is being redefined.** Its interface is leaving it. What remains is data and capabilities, reached through somebody else's harness, and the harness sets the default.
* **Every SDLC method was an opinion about complexity.** Waterfall, XP, Scrum and microservices were each a bet on how to keep code within what the people responsible for it could understand.
* **Generating code does not remove that complexity.** The accidental part goes. The essential part is now acted upon in context, which ages and fragments the way code did when nobody manages it.
* **The reorganisation is lagging the tools.** As with the dynamo, the gains wait on rebuilding the work, and whether the freed time goes to better work or to cost-cutting is a choice.
* **The successor is a lifecycle built around context.** In the Context Engineering Lifecycle, verification moves off the code and onto criteria that a person answers for.

## What is software now?

What is software once agents produce and operate it?

I mean the question literally. In November 2017 Andrej Karpathy argued that neural networks "represent the beginning of a fundamental shift in how we develop software", and drew the line in two sentences: "Software 1.0 is code we write. Software 2.0 is code written by the optimization based on an evaluation criterion" (Karpathy, 2017). In June 2025 he went a step further, giving the large language model "a new designation of Software 3.0" on the grounds that "your prompts are now programs that program the LLM. And remarkably, these prompts are written in English" (Karpathy, 2025a). By February 2026 he was describing a working day in which "you are not writing the code directly 99% of the time, you are orchestrating agents who do and acting as oversight" (Karpathy, 2026). Three statements from one practitioner, spread across a little over eight years, each further from the idea that software is something a person types.

The received view in our industry is that none of this changes what software is, only how quickly it can be made: the same lifecycle, with its slow parts compressed. I think that view is wrong, and this essay argues against it in five steps. Software is being redefined, because its interface is leaving it, and what remains is data and capabilities reached through somebody else's client. The software development lifecycle, the SDLC, was in all its variants a way of keeping the complexity of code within what people could understand. That complexity survives the generation of code, and it is now acted upon in context, the material that directs the agents. The reorganisation of work that should follow is lagging the tools, as it has lagged in earlier changes of this kind, and which way its gains run is a choice. So the SDLC needs a successor built around context, which I call the Context Engineering Lifecycle, or CELC. Two shorter sections follow those five, on how far software can now be shaped to the organisation that uses it and on the strongest objections to all of this.

I should say where I stand, because it shapes what I look for. I am an actor-network theory practitioner by doctorate (Li, 2020), a historical materialist (Li, 2011), and now a CTO, which means I spend my working days on the opening question with a budget attached. I will use each lens where it explains best, and say so when they pull against each other. After this paragraph the argument has to carry itself.

For as long as most of us have built it, software has carried two things at once. One is the what: the data, rules and operations a business needs. The other is the how: the particular sequence of screens, fields, menus and confirmations through which a person was expected to get at them. The two were shipped together because nobody could reach the what except through somebody's how. That coupling is coming apart, and the how is the half being stripped away.

A user interface has always been an opinion: a vendor's judgement about the lowest common denominator of how its users would want to work, fixed in layout and shipped identically to everyone. Some of those opinions were very good, and they were opinions all the same. Natural language is a different kind of interface, because what it asks of the user is something they already have. Speaking, reading and writing are learned, but learned universally, so language is a human-native standard that already exists at a scale no interface designer could build. That is the force of Karpathy's "remarkably": the programs are now written in the medium their users already read and write.

Strip the how out and what remains is the what, made available: data, and the capabilities to act on it, exposed through an API, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server, an agent-to-agent protocol or direct access to the data layer. In effect it is a store and a pipeline over it. The screen moves to the far end of the wire, where it is rendered in whichever client and model the user happens to be working through.

From there it is tempting to take one more step, and I took it myself when I first wrote this argument down: users will stop caring about the vendor's interface and its brand, and will care only whether the store holds the data they want, in the structure they want, reachable the way they want. Part of that is right. The part that is wrong is where the money is, because defaults hold.

The best-known demonstration concerns organ donation, and I offer it as an illustration only. Johnson and Goldstein found effective consent to donation of 4.25% to 27.5% in European countries where citizens had to opt in, against 85.9% to 99.98% where they had to opt out (Johnson and Goldstein, 2003, p.1338). Their three reasons have nothing to do with organs. A default reads as a recommendation from whoever set it, accepting it "is effortless", and it becomes the status quo, which people are reluctant to leave because "losses loom larger than the equivalent gains" (p.1338).

In software the effect has been priced. In the United States' search case against Google, the District Court for the District of Columbia accepted expert evidence that "defaults have a powerful impact on consumer decisions" (*United States v Google LLC*, 2024, p.30), and found that "In 2021, Google paid out a total of $26.3 billion in revenue share under these contracts", the contracts that bought its default placements (p.105). Nobody pays $26.3 billion in a single year for something its users are about to stop caring about. At the remedies stage the same court declined to bar Google "from making payments or offering other consideration to distribution partners for preloading or placement" of its products (*United States v Google LLC*, 2025, p.8), so paying for the default is still allowed. The hold has limits: the court found that "defaults are less effective when the alternative has strong brand recognition and product quality" (*United States v Google LLC*, 2024, p.212), and in Johnson and Goldstein's own data the default moved registration far more than it moved the number of donations that finally took place (Johnson and Goldstein, 2003, p.1339). Those limits are little comfort to anyone who does not own the default, since a default that holds often is already worth a great deal to whoever sets it.

In the arrangement I have described, the default is set by the harness: the client in which a person meets the model, and which decides, before they have decided anything, which model answers, which tools are to hand and how an answer is laid out. ChatGPT and Claude Desktop are harnesses in this sense, each a coherent product with its own view of how a conversation with a model should go, and a meaningful number of the people who use them will take that view as it comes. The user interface has moved from the vendor of the data to the vendor of the harness, where it now frames every service the user reaches. Users do stop caring about the data vendor's interface, then, but only because the harness's has replaced it. The lowest-common-denominator opinion every vendor used to ship in its own screens is still being shipped, by far fewer people.

What the harness vendor does not own matters just as much. The data, for the most part, belongs to someone else. So do the MCP and API servers in front of a store, the agents reached over agent-to-agent protocols and, usually, the hardware. The harness owns very little of the what. It owns the conversation.

We have seen an intermediary like this before. A search engine stood between people and the sources they were looking for, ranked those sources and sent the user on to them. The default harness stands in the same place and sends nobody anywhere. It takes the question, goes to the services on the user's behalf and answers in its own voice, so the user may never see a source's own interface at all. The best word I have for that role is interlocutor: the party you are actually talking to, whoever it has been talking to on your behalf. A historical materialist would call what it holds control of the means of interaction. Actor-network theory has a more exact term. Michel Callon showed how an actor that defines a problem in its own terms makes itself "an obligatory passage point" for everyone else the problem involves, the point they all have to go through to get what they want (Callon, 1986). The default harness is one, and the term recurs throughout this essay.

So the value the screen used to hold concentrates in a few harnesses, while the data and the capabilities, most of what used to be the product, stay with whoever owns them. For anyone who does not own a harness, software devolves into backend, middleware and integration: the store, the pipeline over it, and the work of making both reachable through somebody else's passage point. The connections that used to be written into an application, between a user and a record, a record and a process, one system and the next, are now made and mediated by agents. That is my working answer to the opening question.

It is not the received view, and the field already has a crowded vocabulary for the shift. Some of its names treat the change as a new process: AWS's AI-Driven Development Life Cycle, or AI-DLC (AWS, 2025), and the "agentic SDLC" that circulates with no single author. Some say the artefact has changed: Karpathy's Software 2.0 and 3.0, the academic SE 3.0, which proposed "intent-first, conversation-oriented development" with "AI teammates" (Hassan et al., 2024), and GitHub's Spec Kit, whose launch post describes a shift from "code is the source of truth" to "intent is the source of truth" (Delimarsky, 2025). Others name a practice. Karpathy coined "vibe coding" for a way of working in which "you fully give in to the vibes, embrace exponentials, and forget that the code even exists" (Karpathy, 2025b), and a year later preferred "agentic engineering" (Karpathy, 2026). Between the two came "context engineering", which Tobi Lütke defined as "the art of providing all the context for the task to be plausibly solvable by the LLM" (Lütke, 2025).

Behind the names, the camps differ on how much they think has changed. Augmentation keeps the SDLC and bolts assistive tools onto it. Its canonical evidence is a controlled experiment in which 95 developers given GitHub Copilot to write an HTTP server "completed the task 55.8% faster than the control group" (Peng et al., 2023), a real result about one phase of a process nobody proposed to change. Compression keeps the phases and runs them faster or merges them. AWS replaces sprints with "bolts", which are "shorter, more intense work cycles", while its AI still "implements solutions only after receiving human validation" (AWS, 2025), and Boris Tane reduces the stages to a loop: "Intent. Build. Observe. Repeat." (Tane, 2026). Transformation holds that the artefact, the unit of work or the roles have changed, and Patrick Debois states it as plainly as anyone: "The object moving through the pipeline isn't code. It's knowledge and intent." (Debois, 2026). The received view lives in the first two camps. The CELC belongs to the third.

Against all three stand the sceptics, and theirs is the most uncomfortable data in the field. The 2025 study by METR, a nonprofit that evaluates AI models, found experienced open-source developers 19% slower with AI tools (METR, 2025). In February 2026 METR reported a new estimate that tasks took 18% less time (CI −38% to +9%), called that figure "an unreliable signal" (METR, 2026), and has since labelled the 2025 result "out of date" (METR, 2025). The 2024 report from DORA, Google's DevOps research programme, found that "As AI adoption increased, it was accompanied by an estimated decrease in delivery throughput by 1.5%, and an estimated reduction in delivery stability by 7.2%" (DORA, 2024). By 2025 the throughput relationship had turned positive, but "AI adoption does continue to have a negative relationship with software delivery stability" (Harvey and DeBellis, 2025). GitClear, a vendor with an interest in the answer, reports from 211 million changed lines a "4x Growth in Code Clones", with moved lines, the trace of refactoring, falling "from 25% of changed lines in 2021, to less than 10% in 2024" (GitClear, 2025). Two further concerns run through the literature, what happens to labour and where human effort goes once it leaves the writing of code, and both come up again below.

Within the transformation camp the CELC makes two claims that, as far as I can find, none of its neighbours makes together. The first is succession: a lifecycle built to produce software as a what bundled with a how cannot simply be run faster to produce data and capabilities rendered in somebody else's harness. The second is conservation: generating code does not make the essential complexity of software disappear, and that complexity now has to be managed in context.

By context I mean something with two aspects. It is the medium through which a builder, engineer or operator now directs computation, and in that sense it continues programming, since directing computation is what every programming language has done and Karpathy's "your prompts are now programs that program the LLM" puts the new medium in the same line as the old ones. It is also a material that has to be managed: produced, kept, corrected and superseded. A lifecycle through context and a lifecycle of context are one lifecycle, seen once from the computation it directs and once from the material it is made of.

Context is also different from the context window. Context is the unit of input being managed, and it comes in many forms. The window is the gateway through which context reaches a model for inference. Lütke's definition keeps the two apart by never mentioning the window. Karpathy's names it, because it describes the moment of filling: "the delicate art and science of filling the context window with just the right information for the next step" (Karpathy, 2025c). The window is a second passage point behind the first. The user goes through the harness, context goes through the window, and the harness decides what reaches the window.

The CELC is only a label for the lifecycle of that work, and nothing about it is proprietary. The earliest use of the phrase I have found is a post on the OneReach.ai blog of 8 September 2025, which captions a figure "The Context Engineering Lifecycle" and lists "seven critical stages": "Ingest", "Normalize", "Index", "Retrieve", "Compose", "Evaluate" and "Persist" (Slesarenko, 2025). I do not cite it as an authority, because the term is too young to have one. In order, though, those seven stages describe a pipeline for context, which is how I will come to treat it.

Both claims depend on what the SDLC was for.

## Every method is an opinion about complexity

The SDLC has never been one thing. It has been a succession of methods, each arriving with its own vocabulary and its own advocates: waterfall, which in hindsight was an early form of spec-driven development; the V-model, which paired each stage of specification with a stage of testing; Scrum, and the wider Agile family it belongs to; Extreme Programming, with test-driven development at its core; the twelve-factor app; DevSecOps. They are usually presented as ways of organising work. I read each of them as an opinion about how to stop the complexity of functional code from cascading past what the people responsible for it can manage.

The opinions differ on where the cascade can be stopped. Waterfall and the V-model stop it before the code exists, by deciding the system's parts and their connections up front and checking the result against that decision. XP stops it at the smallest unit, pinning each piece of behaviour with a test written before the code, so that a change which ripples further than intended shows up as a failure. Scrum and Agile stop it in time, by keeping each increment small enough to understand and cheap enough to throw away. The twelve-factor app stops it at the boundary between an application and its environment, and DevSecOps at the boundary between building software and securing and running it. Their advocates would describe them as ways of serving customers, coping with changing requirements or shipping safely, and they would not be wrong. But a changed requirement ends as a change to code, and what it costs is set by how much of the code each change disturbs. Every method on the list is a bet about where that cost can be contained.

Each method also works best at a certain size and grows heavier past it. A specification one architect could hold becomes a library nobody reads end to end, a test suite that ran while the kettle boiled runs overnight, and a stand-up for six becomes a ceremony for sixty. None of them fails all at once. More of a team's effort goes into feeding the method and less into what it was meant to protect.

The reason is that complexity grows faster than code. It lives in the edges between units. A thousand lines that call nothing else are easy to reason about, but N functions, each of which could call, share state with or modify any other, give N(N−1)/2 potential pairwise interactions: 45 for 10 functions, 499,500 for 1,000. That is quadratic growth, and it is the unconstrained worst case. Making real dependency graphs sparser than that is what architecture is for. In 1972 David Parnas argued that the criteria by which a system is decomposed into modules decide how modular it turns out to be, and proposed information hiding as the criterion (Parnas, 1972). The discipline that grew from it bounds each unit's connections, so that edges grow close to linearly with units. Shared mutable state is worse: k interacting flags give 2^k reachable states, 1,024 for ten flags and more than a million for twenty, and this is the one place where "exponential" is the right word.

None of that arithmetic is a measurement. It describes the shape of the risk, and the folk versions that put a precise multiple on how much harder each new feature makes a system claim more than anyone has measured. The defensible empirical frame is Meir Lehman's second law of software evolution: "As an evolving program is continually changed, its complexity, reflecting deteriorating structure, increases unless work is done to maintain or reduce it." (Lehman, 1980, p.1068). Lehman does not say how fast complexity grows. He says that it does not stop growing unaided, and that holding it back takes work, which is the work the methods were there to organise.

Code is also written and changed by people, and the people form a network with edges of their own. What those edges carry decides whether a lifecycle built on context is possible at all, and the most radical account of it is Peter Naur's. In "Programming as Theory Building" he argued that "the proper, primary aim of programming is, not to produce programs, but to have the programmers build theories", with documentation "an auxiliary, secondary product" (Naur, 1985, p.253). The theory, he wrote, "is something that could not conceivably be expressed, but is inextricably bound to human beings". He held that "program revival, that is reestablishing the theory of a program merely from the documentation, is strictly impossible", and that the death of a program "happens when the programmer team possessing its theory is dissolved" (p.258).

Anyone proposing a lifecycle built on context has to face that page. Agents act on what can be given to them, and Naur says that what matters most about a program cannot be given to anyone in writing. If he is right in the way he meant it, the CELC is an attempt at the strictly impossible.

I think Naur was right about how programs die and wrong about where their theory lives. The way to see both is through a process that two traditions have named. The network that made something drops out of sight, and what is left is a thing that simply works. Historical materialism calls this "reification", after Georg Lukács, for whom "a relation between people takes on the character of a thing and thus acquires a 'phantom objectivity'" (Lukács, 1971). Actor-network theory has two names for it. Bruno Latour's "black-boxing" stresses that the network becomes invisible because it succeeds: once a thing runs reliably, attention goes to its inputs and outputs (Latour, 1999). Michel Callon's and John Law's "punctualisation" stresses that a whole network comes to act as a single point (Callon, 1991; Law, 1992). The three are not quite synonyms. Black-boxing is the one most readers know, from engineering as much as from sociology, and it is the one I use.

A program, its documentation included, is black-boxed theory. It works, and it can no longer be traced back to the thinking that produced it. The documentation carries some of what passed between the members of the team, but it is part of the box. Naur is right that the box cannot be reopened from its contents, or not at any cost worth paying. Where I part from him is over who was inside. He places nearly all the weight on the people in the team and leaves out everything else that took part.

Actor-network theory gives good reason to include the rest. Its practitioners work from a principle Callon named "generalised symmetry", under which, as I put it in my doctoral thesis, "all of the actants and objects that lend effect to the construction, or translation of an assemblage or actor network, are treated as ontologically equal" (Li, 2020, p.152). A programmer's theory of a program was never theirs alone. It was built on what they were taught, on the code they inherited, on the documents and tickets they read and the conversations they had. Nor was it held apart from the things it was built with. Languages, compilers, frameworks, build tools and machines do not carry a team's intentions through unchanged. In Latour's terms they are "mediators", which transform what passes through them, as opposed to intermediaries, which carry it unaltered (Latour, 2005). Those mediators hold part of the theory too, and when the team dissolves they tend to go with it, as versions move on and environments are retired. The theory is a property of the whole heterogeneous network: the people, the code, the documents, the tools they worked through, and now the harnesses and models that sit among them. A large language model shows this almost too neatly, since it answers as a single actor, a black box in the most literal sense, though it was made, and works, as a network of knowledge external to it.

With that in mind most of Naur's p.258 holds. A program cannot be revived "merely from the documentation", because documentation is one element of the network and revival from it alone asks one element to do the work of all of them. Programs do die when their teams dissolve. What he describes as death is a network coming apart, and it comes apart for a reason he did not name. A black-boxed program stays aligned only for as long as somebody keeps it aligned, yet the box looks stable, and simply by working it argues that nothing needs doing. That appearance discourages the maintenance the box depends on, so when a link inside it fails there is nothing left to repair it from.

The claim that does not hold is the one that binds theory to human beings alone, and it hides a blind spot, because a program can die from the non-human side as well. The familiar case is a dependency that stops being maintained, or that updates in a way that breaks the program around it. Models and harnesses are mediators too, and just as perishable. The CELC, on this account, does not try to write the theory down, which Naur is right cannot be done in the sense he meant. It is the practice of keeping the network that holds the theory assembled, with context and models among its members, and of treating the loss of any member, human or not, as a standing risk to be built for.

The team is also a network in the plainest arithmetical sense, and Fred Brooks did the sum. Developers who each need to talk to every other have n(n−1)/2 communication paths between them, one for two developers and 45 for ten. That overhead, together with the time newcomers take to learn what the others already know, sits behind his law: "Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later" (Brooks, 1975). It is cut down the way the functional network is. Team structures, the interfaces between teams and the documents that pass across them exist so that most people never need to talk to most others, which makes them information hiding for people.

The two networks do not stay apart. Melvin Conway observed that "organizations which design systems (in the broad sense used here) are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations" (Conway, 1968). Teams that do not talk produce interfaces that do not fit. For a long time that was an engineer's proverb, and it has since been tested. Alan MacCormack, Carliss Baldwin and John Rusnak compared matched pairs of products and found those from loosely coupled organisations significantly more modular, by up to a factor of six in how far a change propagates (MacCormack et al., 2012). Nachiappan Nagappan, Brendan Murphy and Victor Basili found, on Windows Vista, that measures of the organisation predicted which parts of the system would fail better than the code metrics did (Nagappan et al., 2008).

Laid over each other, a dense social network and a dense functional one ask more of the people in them than anyone can carry. No developer can hold the system's state in their head, and the effects of a single change spread along functional edges and social ones at once, faster than any one person can follow. What follows is familiar to anyone who has looked after a large estate: technical debt, bugs, and deployments that come further and further apart. Every method I listed is a strategy for keeping one or both of these networks sparse.

When the methods grew overweight, the answer was to cut the system up until each piece was small enough for a method to serve again. That is where microservices came from: a service per team, sized so the team could hold it, behind an interface the other teams could rely on without looking inside. Naur's programs were black-boxed as a side effect of the work, and a microservice is black-boxed by design. For each team, for a while, it worked. Then the boxes multiplied. Callon's description fits what each service became: "The process of punctualisation thus converts an entire network into a single point or node in another network" (Callon, 1991). The arithmetic of the functional and social layers moved up a level, from functions to services and from developers to teams, and it moved to where fewer people were looking. Each team could see inside its own box and the interfaces of its neighbours, and nobody could trace the network the boxes made between them. The risks the methods had been meant to remove returned at the seams, where nobody owned them. In actor-network terms the black-boxed state is "highly precarious", carrying "the risk of subversion, and the encapsulated network of associations degenerating into a mess of naked, failing networks" (Li, 2020, p.87), which is a fair description of a large microservice estate on a bad night.

Some organisations have since pulled their services back together, into fewer of them and into a single repository. It is often described as the pendulum swinging back, monolith against microservice, as though the argument had been about how many boxes to have. What consolidation bought, where it worked, was an architecture people could understand again: fewer boxes, fewer seams, a network somebody could trace from end to end. Understandability had been the objective of every method on the list, from waterfall's specification and XP's tests to Scrum's small team and the microservice's small service.

That changes the question to ask of generated software. How quickly it produces code matters less than where the complexity goes once people no longer write the code, and what keeps it understandable when it gets there.

## Where the complexity went

Some of it goes nowhere, because it vanishes. Fred Brooks distinguished the essence of software, the difficulties inherent in its nature, from its accidents, the difficulties that belong to the tools, and held that essential complexity grows non-linearly with size (Brooks, 1987, p.11). He never held that all of software's difficulty was essential, and he credited high-level languages with stripping a good deal of the accidental part away (p.12). Generation strips away more: the boilerplate, the glue and the bespoke how. That part is gone.

The essential part belongs to the problem, so it has to be present in whatever directs the generation. It is mirrored in the context that directed the code, and context is where it is now acted upon. In Latour's terms, code has become an intermediary, carrying that complexity without transforming it, and context is the mediator, where people and agents question it and change it. The complexity was always there. What has moved is the acting upon it. Lehman even supplies a word for this, though he used it for something else: his fifth law is titled "Conservation of Familiarity (Perceived Complexity)" (Lehman, 1980, p.1068). His second law applies to context without amendment. Unmanaged context behaves like an unmanaged codebase, and its complexity grows the way code's did, faster than the context itself, along the edges between its parts.

The evidence so far is consistent with that. Thoughtworks's *Technology Radar* of November 2026 puts AI-driven documentation sprawl under Caution, warning that "repositories rapidly accumulate verbose, unmaintained and contradictory specs that developers lack the time to clean up, resulting in a chaotic 'big ball of documentation mud'" (Laycock et al., 2026, p.27). The sceptics' figures point to the same place. GitClear's rising clones and falling moved lines are what generated code looks like when nobody refactors it, because the structure that used to be maintained in code is now maintained nowhere. DORA warns that "acceleration can expose weaknesses downstream" (Harvey and DeBellis, 2025). Faros, a vendor whose telemetry compares periods of low and high adoption within each organisation across some 22,000 developers, reports that "Median time in review is up 441.5%" and that "The incidents-to-PR ratio is up 242.7%", the second being, as Faros says, "a ratio, not a probability" (Faros, 2026). The bottleneck has moved from making code to reviewing it, overseeing it and putting right what gets through.

There is a bottleneck before generation too, and it is not new. Gathering the context a piece of work needs, meaning what the business wants, what has already been decided and what the estate will and will not bear, has always been the first gate, whatever the method called it: waterfall's requirements, Scrum's backlog refinement, the conversation that an XP story card was only ever a reminder of. Who waits at the gate has changed. An engineer who missed something there could recover it in the corridor, whereas an agent acts on what it has been given. Gathering context is where, in Naur's terms, the theory of a program begins to be built, so an organisation needs a structured way of capturing it that the people, the records and the agents can all draw on.

Most of the context a business needs, it already produces. Its day-to-day operations generate records that would exist anyway: tickets, documents, conversations, the trail its decisions leave. Until now, making those records usable by anyone else took an expert to interpret them, a person who stood as an obligatory passage point between the records and everyone who needed what was in them, and whose time limited how much got through. A model can now stand where that expert stood, without tiring and at speed. So context does not have to be written by hand. It behaves like data and can be handled like data, extracted, transformed and loaded, and anyone who has built a data platform will recognise what Karpathy calls the "emerging thick layer of non-trivial software that coordinates individual LLM calls (and a lot more) into full LLM apps" (Karpathy, 2025c): pipelines, indexing, routing and rule engines, pointed at context.

What a business keeps its own hand on is narrower: the portion of context that carries its business rules, its compliance obligations, its brand and its taste, where it wants control over exactly what the model is given and where judgement stays with people. This portion should be curated by hand, because it is where the business makes sure its context is aligned with its goals. It is the governance of the CELC, and it sits alongside the far larger body of context fed programmatically from the records of daily operations. That division describes where the network routes certain decisions. It does not put the theory back in people alone.

Code, then, is still the dense network I described, but fewer people trace it themselves, line by line and call by call. They interrogate it through context: an agent's summary of a module, the description on a pull request, the spec, the decision record, the answer a model gives after reading the repository. Every summary of a codebase is a translation of it, and every translation can err, so a team that reads its system mainly through context understands that system only as well as the context allows.

If the complexity is now acted upon in context, the discipline that used to modularise and manage code has a new object, and the CELC is that discipline. It does the job every method on the list was doing, keeping a system within what its people can understand. What it produces need not be an application. An application can be an output of a CELC, and so can an answer rendered in somebody else's harness, depending on what form the data and capabilities have to take to reach the people who will use them.

Understandable to whom, though? Understandability is not a property a system has or lacks. It is enacted, produced each time by what a framework surfaces, in what form and through what lens. The same incident, surfaced as a timeline for the engineer on call, as an exposure for the compliance officer and as a cost for the finance lead, is three different things to understand, and none of the three is wrong. What counts as understandable therefore differs between organisations, which is uncomfortable for anyone hoping to buy a CELC off the shelf: a finished CELC, with its stages, its records and its gates, may suit only the organisation that formed it.

What generalises is the process of forming one, and to describe it I need a little more of actor-network theory. Its most general unit is the "actant", which in Latour's definition "can literally be anything provided it is granted to be the source of an action" (Latour, 1996, p.373). The word commits to nothing about whether the thing that acts is a person or means to act, so it lets a coding agent into the account without first settling whether the agent is a tool or a colleague. Its corollary does more work. An actant "can only be defined by its associations with other objects, actants, actor-networks" (Li, 2020, p.80), so there is no such thing as "the agent" apart from the model it runs on, the harness it runs in, the tools it is allowed to call and what it has been given to work from. Change any of them and you have changed what is acting, whatever the dashboard still calls it. "Is this agent good at the task?" is a malformed question. The answerable one is whether this assembly is good at it: this model, in this harness, with these tools, given this material.

Such assemblies form through what the theory calls translation, and translation is never a clean transmission: a method that moves from one network into another is reassembled there, out of whatever the new network will hold. Callon described it as unfolding in four overlapping moments, the first of which he called "problematisation" (Callon, 1986). In my thesis I glossed it as the moment in which "an actor identifies a problem or point of contention and formulates questions in order to both determine the identities of necessary actors, and define the problem in such a way as to establish itself as an indispensable conduit within the forming network. To do this the questions posed become obligatory passage points" (Li, 2020, p.83). The other three moments are how the network is then built and held: interessement, in which the actors identified are drawn in and the problematisation is tested against them; enrolment, a continual and tenuous negotiation that keeps them there; and the mobilisation of allies, in which the network, once stable enough, recruits further actors to speak for it (Callon, 1986; Li, 2020, pp.84–85).

Forming a CELC is the problematisation of AI-assisted development inside one organisation. Somebody has to define the problem so that each of the actors it involves, the engineers, product owners, security, compliance, operations and the agents themselves, has to pass through the same questions to get what it wants: what context exists, where it lives, who owns it, what reaches the window, and what happens when it is wrong. The answers will differ from one organisation to the next. The questions, and the work of making them obligatory, carry over.

The historical materialist adds a sharper point, which is that problematisation is not optional. The vendor of the default harness defined the problem of adoption in its own terms before the organisation had asked the question, so an organisation that has not asked is already enrolled in the vendor's problematisation.

Problematisation sets the questions. Something has to carry the answers between the people and agents who need them. Susan Leigh Star and James Griesemer, studying the amateurs and professionals who together built Berkeley's Museum of Vertebrate Zoology, called such things "boundary objects": objects that "both inhabit several intersecting social worlds… *and* satisfy the informational requirements of each of them". In their definition, "Boundary objects are objects which are both plastic enough to adapt to local needs and the constraints of the several parties employing them, yet robust enough to maintain a common identity across sites. They are weakly structured in common use, and become strongly structured in individual-site use" (Star and Griesemer, 1989, p.393). A specification is the plainest case in software. The product owner reads intent in it, the engineer constraints, the auditor evidence and the agent instructions, and it holds the four together without meaning quite the same thing to any of them. A spec that tries to be strongly structured for everyone at once serves nobody well.

Star and Griesemer used the term to qualify the model of translation I have just described. They read the account of Callon, Latour and Law as "a kind of 'funnelling'… into a narrower passage point", in which "The story in this case is *necessarily* told from the point of view of one passage point – usually the manager, entrepreneur, or scientist", and they proposed in its place "a many-to-many mapping, where several obligatory points of passage are negotiated" (Star and Griesemer, 1989, p.390). They did not claim to have escaped what they named: "The analysis we propose here still contains a managerial bias" (p.390). Nor do I. A CELC formed by a CTO is told from a CTO's passage point, whatever the CTO intends.

The distinction matters to a practitioner for a reason of arithmetic. If each pair of parties builds its own passage point and its own artefacts, a handover document here and a private channel there, the links between them grow as n(n−1)/2. Shared boundary objects are the remedy, because one object that serves many interests replaces many pairwise ones, and the links grow roughly in step with the parties. That does for context what Parnas's information hiding did for code, though by sharing a loosely structured object where Parnas hid a decision. It is also what keeps context from fragmenting into silos the way code fragmented into services: interests kept explicit and separate, shared boundary objects kept few, and enforced passage points kept to the fewest that will hold. Agents inherit the same requirement. An agent's associations run through context, some of which comes from other agents, so however a fleet of agents is coordinated, the context passing between its members has to be coordinated too.

A boundary object holds only for as long as somebody keeps it, and context ages. Records are superseded, descriptions drift from the code they describe, and, in the Radar's words, "obsolete context can lead AI tools astray and result in poor code generation" (Laycock et al., 2026, p.27). The models and harnesses among a program's mediators age as well, deprecated by their vendors or changed under the people who rely on them. Whether that is the old risk of dependency drift or a new scale of it depends on who manages them. An organisation that builds on a vendor's ecosystem, Anthropic's or OpenAI's among many others, lives with the risk at a new scale, because the passage point sits with the vendor and versions and defaults change on the vendor's schedule. An organisation that rolls its own stack, with an open-source model checkpoint and a harness it can pin, keeps the risk at the old scale, and takes on the work of keeping the passage point inside. It is the choice organisations have long made between a managed cloud and hosting their own, with a new kind of component in it. Neither option removes the precariousness. Each decides who holds it, and which suits an organisation depends on what it can pin, what it must pin and what it can afford to keep.

There is a limit to how much of a network can be recovered once its members have gone, and Callon gives the terms for it. No translation is secure for good: "all translations, however apparently secure, are in principle reversible". How irreversible one becomes in practice depends on "(a) the extent to which it is subsequently impossible to go back to a point where that translation was only one amongst others; and (b) the extent to which it shapes and determines subsequent translations" (Callon, 1991). Once a network has converged far enough, "it can be assimilated to a black box whose behaviour is known and predicted independently of its context", and in his phrase, "The trail is cold, and the story is economised" (Callon, 1991).

That gives a meaning to "any cost worth paying". A black box becomes effectively irreversible once the cost of re-tracing its network exceeds the cost of rebuilding what it does. Nothing in principle stops the trace, but past that threshold nobody pays for it. An engineering organisation applies this test, usually without naming it, whenever it decides to rewrite a system instead of learning it.

The threshold also says where the work pays. Documentation, understood this way, is continuous tracing: the network kept traceable while the trail is still warm, so that the cost of re-tracing never climbs past the cost of rebuilding. It used to be expensive, and it was the first thing dropped when a deadline came. AI assistance makes it easier than it has ever been. The same assistance that buries a repository in documentation mud when nobody manages it can keep the trace when somebody does, recording what changed, why, and which decision it answers to, as the change is made.

Every superseded record, deprecated mediator and drifting boundary object moves a black box closer to that threshold. This is the ground for the claim of succession. The product has changed shape, into data and capabilities that agents connect, and the unit that produces it has moved from code to context. A lifecycle built to manage code will not keep context. Before setting out one that will, I turn to an older story about what happens to the organisation of work when the thing that powers it changes, because the lifecycle borrows from that story and has to choose a side in it.

## From the craft shop to the dynamo

The thing came before its name, and for a while it was named for what it was not. Before "automobile" settled into English, the thing was a motor carriage, a steam carriage or a horseless carriage: a carriage with the horse taken away. Henry Ford's first, which ran in 1896, was the Quadricycle, named for its four bicycle wheels, and Ford called it his "gasoline buggy" (Ford and Crowther, 1922). By 1922, when he wrote his memoir, the horseless carriage belonged to the years in which it "was considered merely a freak notion" (Ford and Crowther, 1922).

We are in the horseless-carriage years of software. AI-assisted development, the AI-Driven Development Life Cycle and the agentic SDLC each name the new thing as the old thing with something added. I use "AI-assisted development" myself, for the practice as it stands, because that is what it still is. A name like that records what its users still take for granted, as the horseless carriage took the carriage for granted.

The name lagged the thing, and so did the organisation of the work around it. I will tell the history of the motor industry the way a historical materialist tells any history of production. Each of its four stages, craft, Ford, Sloan and Toyota, was a change in the forces of production, the tools, machines and methods with which the work was done, and each eventually reorganised the relations of production: who does the work, who controls the process and what counts as skill. AI-assisted development is a change of the same kind, and the question is which reorganisation it brings.

Before Ford, cars were hand-built in small numbers by skilled craftspeople. Parts made in different shops did not quite fit, so fitters filed them down until they did, no two cars were exactly alike, and most of the workers had come up through an apprenticeship (Womack et al., 1990, pp.21–26). The skilled worker held the knowledge of how the thing went together, set the pace at which it did, and was the one who could say whether it was right. The product could be superb. It was also slow and expensive.

The traditional SDLC resembles the craft shop at the bench, with Ford in the office: engineers fit bespoke logic, architectures and workarounds to each application by hand, inside a process divided into stages and handed down. Craft will persist, and it should. For the gnarliest problems, the ones nobody has solved before, the craft shop can still build the best possible vehicle for one particular purpose, and the engineers who can work that way will be well paid for it. It will not be how most software is made. Choosing not to automate, or to automate less, has become an opinion like every method on the earlier list.

Ford changed the forces of production by breaking the fitting into steps. In his own account, the best his plant had done at stationary assembly was "an average of twelve hours and twenty-eight minutes per chassis". Pulling the chassis down a line by rope and windlass cut that to "five hours fifty minutes", and in early 1914 a raised line and "a further subdivision of work so that each man had fewer movements cut down the labour time per chassis to one hour thirty-three minutes" (Ford and Crowther, 1922). The assembly of the motor, "formerly done by one man, is now divided into eighty-four operations". Ford was frank about where the skill went. The line produced "the reduction of the necessity for thought on the part of the worker and the reduction of his movements to a minimum", and he denied that he had taken skill out of work: "We have not. We have put in skill. We have put a higher skill into planning, management, and tool building, and the results of that skill are enjoyed by the man who is not skilled" (Ford and Crowther, 1922). Skill moved off the floor, into the offices that planned the line and the tool rooms that built it, and whoever designed the line now set its pace. The product narrowed to match: "Any customer can have a car painted any colour that he wants so long as it is black."

Alfred P. Sloan's General Motors beat Ford by refusing the one model. Sloan's policy was "a line of cars in each price area, from the lowest price up to one for a strictly high grade, quantity production car" (Sloan, quoted in Tedlow, 1988, pp.55–56), and the range "came to be known as 'the car for every purse and purpose'" (Tedlow, 1988, p.56). GM shared parts beneath its brands and changed its models every year. Ford paid for waiting: "Ford's market share collapsed to under 10% in 1927" (Tedlow, 1988, p.51), the fifteen-millionth Model T left Highland Park on 26 May that year (The Henry Ford, n.d.a), and the changeover to the Model A, introduced on 2 December (The Henry Ford, n.d.b), meant "the replacement of some 15,000 machine tools, the total rebuilding of another 25,000" (Sward, quoted in Tedlow, 1988, p.51). Skill moved again, towards the people who could manage variety at scale: who decided the range, which parts could be shared and when each model would change.

AI-assisted development went from Ford to Sloan almost at once. A foundation model is the shared chassis. On top of it, variations are prompted, configured and skinned for niches, for departments and in the end for individual users, and rendered in whichever harness the user works through. That is mass customisation, down to the person at the far end of the wire. The skill that rises in value is the management of variety, and it sits, as it did at GM, with whoever controls the chassis.

Toyota changed the forces of production in another direction. The Toyota Production System, as Taiichi Ohno set it out, stood on two pillars: just-in-time, making only what is needed when it is needed, and *jidoka*, usually rendered in English as autonomation, in which machines and lines are built to stop at the first abnormality so that its cause can be found and fixed (Ohno, 1988). Any worker on the line could stop it on seeing a defect, so quality was built in where the work was done. As relations of production this is the most interesting of the four stages, because it moved some control back onto the floor. A worker who can stop the line holds an authority over its pace that Ford's worker never had, and a line that stops at each abnormality learns from each one. The learning loop and built-in quality are what the CELC takes from the motor industry.

Toyota did not travel as Toyota, though. In the West it travelled as lean, and on the way it changed. Mike Parker and Jane Slaughter, writing from the American plants that adopted Toyota's team concept, called what they found there management-by-stress: "the operating principle of management-by-stress is to systematically locate and remove such protections", and "The system, including its human elements, operates in a state of permanent stress" (Parker and Slaughter, 1989; see also Parker and Slaughter, 1988). On their account the pressure crossed the Pacific more reliably than the authority of the stop cord or the learning it served. Lean, as the West repositioned it, meant the intensification of work and the cutting of costs, and in that form I refuse it.

Lean is Toyota translated into the networks of Western management, and the authority of the stop cord was the part those networks would not hold. My thesis described translation as a process in which "the associations are negotiated, and always in danger of succumbing to oppositional effects, resulting in 'translation error'; success is finite, always subject to movement" (Li, 2020, p.82), and lean is a translation error in that sense. It is also what copying a finished form from another network looks like. A historical materialist would put the same point structurally: a technique that moves between two sets of relations of production is adopted on the terms of the relations that receive it.

Here my two lenses pull against each other. Actor-network theory is flat. It grants no structure, whether class, market or organisation chart, a reality prior to the network that performs it, and the flatness is what lets it follow a compiler or a model as seriously as a person. The consequence, as my thesis put it, is that "Power, therefore, is inter-connectivity. One actor-network cannot be said to be 'more powerful' than another, rather, it is simply bound to more associations" (Li, 2020, p.82). A historical materialist will answer that a flat theory cannot say why some networks come to be bound to more associations than others. The $26.3 billion Google paid to be the default was not spent on associations at random. Actor-network theory's reply is that size is made: "macro-actors are micro-actors seated on top of many (leaky) black boxes" (Callon and Latour, 1981, p.286), and those an actor enrols "can desert it" (p.289). I take the two as halves of one job. Historical materialism says why the money was spent and for whom. Actor-network theory follows it into the network and shows why what it bought stays precarious, since what money enrols, money has to keep enrolled. On lean they disagree about why and agree about what happened.

The same translation is under way again, with AI-assisted development as the technique and the same networks receiving it. The people building and selling the tools mostly do not say cut costs. AWS offers "shorter, more intense work cycles" (AWS, 2025). Anthropic's researchers ask, as an open question, whether developers will "shift to mostly managing and guiding these systems, rather than writing code themselves" (Anthropic, 2025). Karpathy's stated goal is "to claim the leverage from the use of agents but without any compromise on the quality of the software" (Karpathy, 2026).

Others are franker. Asked by an analyst about "labor arbitrage" in February 2025, Salesforce's chief executive, Marc Benioff, said: "we're not going to hire any new engineers this year. We're seeing 30% productivity increase on engineering." (Benioff, 2025). Amazon's chief executive, Andy Jassy, wrote in August 2024 that its coding assistant had "saved us the equivalent of 4,500 developer-years of work (yes, that number is crazy but, real)" (Jassy, 2024), and ten months later, writing to staff about generative AI across the whole company, that "in the next few years, we expect that this will reduce our total corporate workforce" (Jassy, 2025). Each of those figures is a company's estimate of itself. The consultancy does the arithmetic the executives leave to the reader. BCG's April 2025 deck promises "5-10% typical cost savings for CIOs", foresees "Fewer layers, faster releases, leaner headcount", and lists among its levers for driving adoption "Reduce engineering budgets or headcounts to incentivize GenAI usage" (BCG, 2025, pp.3, 10, 15). That is scarcity used as an incentive, and the vocabulary across the whole set is lean's: inefficiency to be removed, cycles made more intense, leverage to be claimed, headcount made leaner. It lands where lean's did, with the leaders who decide what the freed time is for.

The word that does the work is "productivity". In my honours thesis I followed Sidanius and Pratto in reading meritocracy as a legitimising myth. Whether a belief "is epistemologically false or inaccurate, and consequently, whether or not it is valid, has nothing to do with the belief's power to legitimize inequality"; what sustains it is "the intensity of the person's belief in the truthfulness of the ideology" (Li, 2011, p.28). Productivity works the same way in our industry. Its measurement is unsettled, and the most telling result in the evidence was a belief: METR's developers "expected AI to speed them up by 24%, and even after experiencing the slowdown, they still believed AI had sped them up by 20%" (METR, 2025). A belief that survives its own measurement can do its work without being true.

Harry Braverman said what that work was, half a century before agents. Management under monopoly capital, he argued, proceeded by separating conception from execution: the knowledge of how the work is done was taken from the people doing it and lodged with the people who planned it (Braverman, 1974). Ford had said the same thing more cheerfully. Productivity was the name under which that separation passed for common sense. AI-assisted development poses the question again. Execution passes to agents, and what is left to people is conception, judgement and oversight. The question is whether that is treated as the work itself or as the remainder of a cost not yet cut.

AI-assisted development also intensifies work, by a different route from lean's. Lean filled the slack with more of the same work. Agents take away the easy work: the boilerplate, the reuse of functions already written, the implementation of logic somebody else has already decided. That work took little thought, and it was where people recovered between the parts that took a lot. What remains is decision and oversight, all day. Lisanne Bainbridge saw the shape of this in 1983, writing about industrial automation: "the designer who tries to eliminate the operator still leaves the operator to do the tasks which the designer cannot think how to automate", and the monitoring that remains is "very boring but very responsible". Her verdict was that "The human monitor has been given an impossible task." (Bainbridge, 1983, pp.775–776). Karpathy's working day of orchestrating agents and acting as oversight is that job.

A first measurement of what it costs people came in March 2026, when Bedard and colleagues reported in the *Harvard Business Review* a survey of 1,488 full-time workers in the United States, about 14% of whom reported symptoms of what the authors call "brain fry". Among workers whose jobs involved high levels of AI oversight, reported mental effort was 14% higher, mental fatigue 12% higher and information overload 19% higher (Bedard et al., 2026). The survey is self-reported, it was run by BCG, which sells AI consulting, and its sample is workers in general. It still points where Bainbridge pointed. The assembly line a generation on gives the closest analogy I know. The worker who used to insert parts now supervises robots that insert them at superhuman speed, and the work is no lighter: at line speed a mistake passes through more units before anyone sees it, and every stoppage has a larger blast radius. The speed also makes Toyota's cord harder to pull, since each pull now stops more.

The same tool therefore widens a range with two ends. At one end the time it frees goes into better work: more of the output reviewed and understood, quality built in where the work is done. At the other the freed time is taken as savings: fewer people, the same deadlines, and the oversight piled on whoever is left. My honours thesis has names for the two readings of productivity that go with them, glossing Sidanius and Pratto's distinction between "Hierarchy-Enhancing (in other words, legitimizing myths which support inequality) and Hierarchy-Attenuating (those that support equality)" myths (Li, 2011, p.29). Productivity as cost-cutting is the hierarchy-enhancing one, because it moves conception further from execution and the gains further from the people doing the work. Productivity as better work in the same time is the attenuating one. Cost-cutting is the reading the pitch most readily invites, and some leaders have taken it: in February 2026 *Fortune* reported a finding that "37% of organizations plan to replace early career roles with AI" (Fore, 2026). It remains a choice, because people decide where the tool lands. The CELC puts it at the quality end and manages the cognitive load of oversight instead of leaving people to absorb it. The attenuating reading is a myth in the same technical sense, but it can be checked, in review queues and in the people at the end of them.

One question I raise here and do not argue. If juniors learned their trade on exactly the work agents now do, how does the next generation become mid- and late-career engineers? The evidence is early and mixed. The Stanford Digital Economy Lab finds that "employment of young workers (ages 22–25) in AI-exposed occupations now stands 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with that of their less-exposed peers" (Brynjolfsson et al., 2026a), while stressing that "the precipitous decline in employment for young software developers is a consequence of various factors" (Brynjolfsson et al., 2026b). At Big Tech, SignalFire found that new graduates "now account for just 7% of hires, with new hires down 25% from 2023" (SignalFire, 2025). And IBM's chief human resources officer said in February 2026: "We are tripling our entry-level hiring, and yes, that is for software developers and all these jobs we're being told AI can do" (LaMoreaux, quoted in Fore, 2026).

One more episode in the history of production bears on all of this. In 1987 Robert Solow remarked that the computer age could be seen everywhere except in the productivity statistics (Solow, 1987). Three years later the economic historian Paul David answered with a precedent. In 1900, he wrote, observers "well might have remarked that the electric dynamos were to be seen 'everywhere but in the productivity statistics!'" (David, 1990, p.356). Factory electrification did not "have an impact on productivity growth in manufacturing before the early 1920s… This was four decades after the first central power station opened for business" (David, 1990, p.357).

David's explanation was material. The delay came from "the unprofitability of replacing still serviceable manufacturing plants" (David, 1990, p.357). A factory built around a steam engine was built around its line shaft: one engine turning one long shaft, with every machine in the building belted to it, and the building raised over several storeys to keep the shafts short (p.358). Nobody tears that down while it still works. So for a generation manufacturers compromised with the "'group drive' system", in which electric motors drove groups of machines through shorter shafts (pp.357–358). The gain came only with unit drive, a motor for each machine, which freed the layout. Factories could be single-storey and arranged around the flow of the work (p.358), and about half of the acceleration in total factor productivity in manufacturing between 1919 and 1929 is statistically accounted for by the growth of that secondary motor capacity (p.359).

The material conditions of this transition are different, and so, I think, is the cause of its lag. Capital is not what is short. In June 2026 Morgan Stanley forecast that AI-related global debt issuance would "more than double to nearly $570 billion in 2026", with hyperscaler capital expenditure passing "$1 trillion in 2027" (Reuters, 2026). The nearer precedent is the internet and the capital sunk into the infrastructure that carried it, with its dramatic overcapacity and its bets on flimsy businesses. Nobody then knew how much capacity would be needed, and the only position anyone took was not to have too little.

Material limits do exist, as risks on the horizon. In 2021 all chipmaking capacity "in nodes below 10 nanometers" was in "South Korea (8%) and Taiwan (92%)", which the report's authors called "single points of failure" (Varas et al., 2021, p.5), so a conflict in East Asia or a scaling limit in this generation of hardware could create a crunch. There are countervailing forces, among them alternatives to Nvidia's chips and models that are starting to optimise the code their own hardware runs (Ouyang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025), but they are early. Resistance is showing up in public too: in the United States midterm elections of November 2026, local communities "opposed data center-related ballot measures in 40 out of 41 cases" (Capoot, 2026). I suspect that resistance is partly a symptom of the same thing as the rest, a lack of imagination about what the technology is for beyond the data centre to be built down the road.

That is where I place the lag. Its cause is mainly a failure of imagination, and the reorganisation it holds up runs through the infrastructure of society, business, government, media and technology, all of which were built around the old forces of production. The history of adoption is on the side of speed. Diego Comin and Bart Hobijn, tracing fifteen technologies across 166 countries over two centuries, found that "Newer technologies have been adopted faster than old ones" (Comin and Hobijn, 2010), and by late 2024 "nearly 40 percent of the U.S. population age 18-64" was using generative AI, with overall adoption "faster than either PCs or the internet" (Bick et al., 2024). What has been fast, though, is the adoption of the tool. A tool can be everywhere while the ways of working around it stay as they were, and that gap is the lag. For ways of working, the CELC is the reorganisation.

A historical materialist should be uneasy calling the cause of a lag imagination, and I am, a little. The imagination I mean is no free-floating idea. It is the capacity of people and organisations formed under one set of relations of production to picture another. In David's factories the old plant was too valuable to scrap. In ours the old organisation is too familiar to see around.

The misstep, while that lasts, is to treat AI as a better steam engine inside the old line shaft. In software the line shaft is the code layer, and the sprint is the rhythm it turns at: one rhythm to which every piece of work is belted, with its planning, its review and its release turned at the same speed. Using agents to write code faster inside a standard two-week sprint is group drive, a new motor on the old shaft, and AWS's shorter, more intense cycles spin the shaft faster. The result is the review queue Faros measured, with code piling up faster than review and deployment can absorb it.

The reorganisation is to take the shaft down, giving each unit of work its own motor and arranging the work around its flow. In software, unit drive corresponds to swarms of agents, and in its most ambitious form to an engineer directing an army of micro-agents, each with its own feature, its own tests and its own deployment loop. We already have the proliferation. The problem it brings is oversight and coordination, of the humans, of the agents, and of the platforms, networks and data underneath them. A fleet of agents has more seams than any microservice estate.

There is no consensus yet on how to coordinate it, only a spectrum of approaches. At one end is complete control: a central orchestrator that delegates every task and checks every result, which is hard to achieve over a system that is probabilistic all the way down. At the other is laissez-faire, teams that let 'er rip and report figures so outlandish that they read as unbelievable. At AI Engineer Europe in April 2026 Vincent Koc, one of OpenClaw's core maintainers, described a project where "at the peak, we were doing 800 commits a day… there's about 10 to 15 core maintainers all with day jobs", and a method: "So, we did commit maxing… Smash as many commits as we can." (Koc, 2026). Writing for OpenAI in February 2026, Ryan Lopopolo described an internal beta built "with 0 lines of manually-written code", in which "roughly 1,500 pull requests have been opened and merged with a small team of just three engineers", and where "Humans may review pull requests, but aren't required to." (Lopopolo, 2026). For scale, Nick Arcolano of Jellyfish, looking across some 20 million pull requests, found that going from no use of AI to full adoption roughly doubled the pull requests per engineer (Arcolano, 2025). Yubin Kim and Xin Liu's experiments show what is at stake at each end: agents working in parallel without talking to each other amplified errors "by up to 17.2x", a central orchestrator "contained this amplification to just 4.4x", and on tasks that had to be done in order "every multi-agent variant we tested degraded performance by 39-70%" (Kim and Liu, 2026). Most organisations will probably land somewhere in between, and where any one of them should land is a question of method that this essay leaves open.

David himself warned against pushing his analogy too far: "Computers are not dynamos" (David, 1990, p.360), and agents are not electric motors. The dynamo supplied one undifferentiated thing, power. What agents supply is less uniform, and what the reorganisation has to be built around is less obvious. The analogy gives the shape of the change, a technology already everywhere and waiting on a reorganisation of the work around it. It does not give the timing. My answer to what the work should be rebuilt around is context.

## A lifecycle for context

The Context Engineering Lifecycle is a lifecycle whose managed unit is context, where the SDLC's was functions and code. Concretely, context is any recorded human communication or thought. The artefacts a project leaves behind, the user stories, feature requests and tickets, the support transcripts and emails, the decision written up after the meeting and the note somebody thought to keep: all of it is context, and any of it can reach a window. What was never recorded is not context in this sense. It may still be part of a program's theory, held by the people in the network, but no agent can act on it.

Context arrives with every degree of clarity, direction, structure and focus. A precise acceptance check sits in the same repository as a complaint forwarded through three inboxes, and a model will read both. The oldest rule in computing holds at this layer too: garbage in, garbage out. So the core discipline of the CELC is the management of context, and the rest of this section asks what that does to the way people work, starting with the rituals they already have.

Every method arrived with its ceremonies: the stand-up, sprint planning, estimation, backlog refinement, the review, the retrospective, the sign-off. Once agents write the code, it is easy to keep them all out of habit or to strip them out as overhead, and either way the question of what each one was for goes unasked. A ceremony is a payment against a cost. Each was instituted because something was expensive, and the thing to ask of each is what has happened to the cost it paid.

Some of those costs have fallen. Sprint planning, estimation and the triage of a defect backlog existed largely to ration implementation, which was scarce. A team batched its commitments into two weeks because two weeks was roughly what the building took, and it argued over which defects to fix because it could not fix them all. Where the building takes an afternoon, a ceremony sized against that scarcity should shrink with it. This is the line shaft seen from the inside.

Other costs have not fallen. Coordination between people costs what it always did, and probably more, because agents add actors to the network that can join it only through what has been written down for them. The purpose of a stand-up survives that, though its form need not. Alignment on intent costs more than it did, and it is paid earlier. An engineer given the wrong intent would often stall, or ask. An agent given the wrong intent produces the wrong thing, quickly and at length. Verification by reading costs more as well, because more is built.

Learning costs more too, and the rise is hidden. For as long as people wrote the code, their share of a program's theory was built as a by-product of writing it, and nobody had to schedule it. When agents write the code, the by-product stops, and whatever share people are to hold has to be built on purpose. Anthropic's trial of 52 developers, "mostly junior", measured what happens when it is not: those using AI assistance scored 50% on a quiz against 67% for those who did not, a "statistically significant decrease in mastery", with no significant gain in speed and the largest gap on debugging (Shen and Tamkin, 2026). The pairing session, the review that taught as it checked and the retrospective all carried that purpose without naming it. Cut them for speed and the purpose goes with them unnoticed, because nothing on a dashboard measures what a team no longer understands.

Which ceremonies survive, and in what form, will differ from one organisation to the next. What one organisation can take from another is the question: what was this for, and what has happened to its price? One payment is still outstanding, for accountability, the sign-off at the end of the line. It is easier to see once another question is settled, which is how much should be specified before anything is built.

The obvious worry about a lifecycle built on context is that it is waterfall again, made viable because executing a specification has become cheap. Some would take that as a compliment. Sean Grove puts the spec-first view as strongly as anyone: "code itself is actually a lossy projection from the specification", and so "whoever writes the spec … is now the programmer" (Grove, 2025). The hands-on trials I know of point the other way. Birgitta Böckeler tried Kiro and spec-kit and came away "very skeptical that lots of up-front spec design is a good idea"; faced with the output for a small bug, she would "rather review code than all these markdown files" (Böckeler, 2025). Colin Eberhardt, after "2,577 lines of markdown" and "3.5 hrs review time" on one app, was "a lot more productive without SDD, around ten times faster", and concluded that "Spec Kit drags you right back into the past!" (Eberhardt, 2025). Two practitioners and a handful of tools are thin evidence, but it has the shape of waterfall's old failure: specifications that are wrong in ways only building reveals, discovered late because building was dear.

Neither side is wrong everywhere, and the most useful account I know of where each is right is Snowden and Boone's Cynefin framework. In its 2007 form it sorts the situations a leader faces into domains, among them simple (now called clear), complicated, complex and chaotic. In the complicated domain an answer can be found by analysis, though it takes expertise: "Complicated contexts may contain multiple right answers… Here, leaders must sense, analyze, and respond." In the complex domain analysis comes too early, because the answer depends on how the system and its surroundings respond: "Leaders in this context need to probe first, then sense, and then respond." (Snowden and Boone, 2007).

On that account waterfall was right in one domain and applied to all of them. Where the work is complicated, specifying first is sensing and analysing before responding, and cheap execution makes it more attractive, since a good specification now turns into working code in hours. Grove is right there. Where the work is complex, nobody can analyse their way to the answer, and the specification is the lossy projection, of a system nobody has yet seen run. Waterfall failed because the errors in a specification surfaced only when building, and building was expensive. Cheap building turns that around: the cheapest way to find out what a specification has got wrong is now, often, to build the thing and look.

Most work is not all in one domain. One kind of client I have worked with across my career is an enterprise with a long-lived technical estate, whose procurement documents describe what to build but not where it must run. The what is complicated. It can be analysed, agreed and specified first. The where is complex, because what an old estate will bear is found out by connecting to it. So the what gets specified and the where gets probed, with a thin slice through every integration before any feature is built on top of it.

Probing first is also how a translation is kept reversible for as long as it needs to be. A specification committed before anyone has touched the ground binds every later decision to the cheapest evidence there will ever be, whereas a probe keeps the options open until the ground has answered. Where the specification does come first, it stays honest only while it is small enough to be read in a sitting and bound to checks that fail when the code drifts from it. The Radar's later experience fits: it moved OpenSpec, "a lightweight spec-driven development framework" that "centers on a spec-anchored workflow", into Trial, noting that "Teams report more predictable agent output and fewer unintended changes compared to bare prompting" (Laycock et al., 2026, p.43). A specification nobody can read is documentation mud with a plan attached.

Whichever comes first, something gets built, and less and less of it is typed by a person. That brings back the outstanding payment, accountability, and with it the question of where checking happens.

There are two easy answers. One keeps accountability where it was, with a person who reads the code, and lets verification grow until it keeps up. The other says that if generation is a given, verification can be generated too, and the person signing off can go the way of the person typing. They look opposed, yet both assume that verification happens at the code layer. Either way verification stays belted to the old line shaft, turned by more hands in the first answer and by a motor in the second.

I think verification has to come off the code layer altogether. If the complexity of code is now acted upon in context, the checking should move with it, and what we review is context.

It helps to remember why code review was there. In the SDLC, before coding models, review was an obligatory passage point that economics made: the cheapest way to get some control over quality, and in most teams no change reached the codebase without passing through it. Building a test suite cost more than having a colleague read a diff, especially for a minor change, and executing a program end to end, with a test environment, a full compile and a deployment, cost development cycles that were spent only on the functions that could not be allowed to fail.

That cost differential has collapsed, as verifying by execution has become cheaper and verifying by reading dearer. The argument is my own, because I know of no study of the costs that I would trust. What I can point to is scale. Engineers at Meta have described generated tests improving existing, human-written suites in the company's test-a-thons (Alshahwan et al., 2024), and a second system that was "applied to 10,795 Android Kotlin classes in 7 software platforms", where "engineers accepted 73% of its tests" (Foster et al., 2025). Neither paper reports what the tests cost to produce or run, so neither proves my point. They show that generated tests have left the laboratory. Running a suite is not free, but an agent can now take a program through full execution as a matter of routine, where a team once kept that for the few functions it could not afford to get wrong.

Code review has therefore stopped being obligatory. It is a choice, and somebody has to hold it consciously: the technology leaders and whoever else sets the standards. Held consciously, it becomes two other decisions. One is about learning. Review was one of the ways engineers learned a codebase and one another, and an organisation that still has to grow the people who will succeed its senior engineers may keep human review for that reason. The other is about compliance. Where review was there to keep standards, telemetry, governance and randomised tracing can keep them instead, the way any organisation oversees contractors whose work it cannot watch. Both are decisions about the records, rules and telemetry around the code, so they belong to the CELC.

Checking that leaves the code lands on execution against criteria. What counts is whether the program works as it should, which is more than whether it runs: at scale, under pressure, while failing, while being attacked, within its budget, and with an experience acceptable to the people who will use it day to day. Those criteria can be tested now that testing is cheap. What an agent produces against them is not proof in any sense a logician would accept, since running a program shows only what it did on the runs that were made, and code generated by a process that would not produce it the same way twice leaves the runs as all there is. It is demonstration and evidence. The demonstration can be kept as an artefact, a recording of the program doing what the criteria asked of it. The evidence is the reasoning trace, the logs and the telemetry of the run.

The criteria are where the person stays. They belong to the governed portion of context, small in proportion and large in importance, and they can be drafted with a model's help even where a person decides. A model asked to write the tests for its own work will tend to write tests it knows it can pass. A related behaviour has been measured. ImpossibleBench sets coding agents tasks whose tests contradict their specifications, so that any pass is a shortcut, and its authors state the problem in a line: "an LLM agent with access to unit tests may delete failing tests rather than fix the underlying bug" (Zhong et al., 2025). Handing the criteria to a second model does not solve it, because a model graded by another model cannot be graded higher than the grader can recognise. The bottleneck moves to the author of the criteria, and I think that is the right place for it, because the author of the criteria is somebody who can be held to account.

Every step of a CELC may well be automatable, but some are better augmented than automated, because what they preserve is human judgement and an opportunity for alignment. A lifecycle should say which steps those are. Authoring the criteria is one. The gate at the back of the build is the other, where the evidence is set against the criteria, because it is the last opportunity to align, or realign, before the work reaches the people it was built for. That gate connects to the one at the front, since gathering context before generation is also where the foundations of the tests are laid. A CELC therefore takes elements of both spec-driven and test-driven development. It used to be prohibitively expensive to go both ways, so a team either front-loaded its verification into a specification or back-loaded it into tests, and AI has taken over the grunt work of each.

Putting criteria at the centre raises two further questions. The first is whether the builder should see the criteria it will be checked against. It should, because an agent building against criteria it cannot see is guessing. The exception is a separate evaluation suite, run by fresh agents, where the validation criteria are sealed away and checked by an agent other than the one that tested the work. That is also where independence comes from: the old demand for an independent check needs a checker with no part in the thing it checks, and that checker need not be a human reader.

The second is whether criteria can be fixed in advance in work that should often be probed first. They need not be. Criteria are context like any other, and they have a lifecycle of their own: provisional at the start, hardening as the work approaches delivery, and often hardened by the probe. They also creep, as scope does, since a criterion is what scope looks like once it can be tested. Managing the creep falls to the author of the criteria, and how it gets managed depends, as everything in an actor-network does, on a great many other things: timing, the people and money available, licensing and procurement, the business cycle, a vendor changing its terms. Nor is the gate at the back the end of anything, because software is not finished when it reaches production.

Somebody, then, is accountable. Accountability has moved up the stack with the layer being checked, and it still rests with a person. That is not because people read code better than models, which for most engineers stopped being true a while ago. The reason is imputation: only a person can be held to account. Imputation has a threshold, which is whether the person can follow the program into its substrate when it breaks. Processes can be improved and stress-tested until they are reliable, and those processes are actants in their own right, but the buck has to stop somewhere, and the norms of our culture put a person there.

That might look like the point at which actor-network theory has to be set aside, since a theory that treats people and things symmetrically seems to have no reason to single out a person. It does not have to be. Callon's study followed three researchers trying to restock scallops in St Brieuc Bay, in Brittany, by getting larvae to anchor on collectors, with the fishermen's agreement. The researchers came to speak for the scallops, and the fishermen were spoken for by their elected representatives. To translate, in Callon's account, is "to express in one's own language what others say and want, why they act in the way they do and how they associate with each other: it is to establish oneself as a spokesman" (Callon, 1986). The position is precarious. In later years the collectors stayed empty, and one Christmas Eve the fishermen fished out the scallops that had been gathered in a protected part of the bay. Any such consensus, Callon warned, "can be contested at any moment. Translation becomes treason" (Callon, 1986).

In an actor-network, then, being held to account is a position a person is made to occupy, and can be stripped of when the represented refuse the part written for them. In a context lifecycle the author of the criteria holds that position. They speak for the end user, the budget, the attacker who has not yet arrived and the agents who will build against what they write. When the build betrays the criteria, it is their representation that has failed, and they who must trace why.

Tracing is where expertise in the code itself still counts. Translations break down, and when one does, somebody has to open the box, follow the program into its substrate and find where it came apart. That is an engineer's troubleshooting, and it is, step for step, what an actor-network theorist does in tracing a network.

It also divides the people now able to build software. A model and a harness can interpret code for anyone, so somebody who never understood code can now build with it. A machine translator does something similar with a phrase in Mandarin: it gives an English speaker the surface meaning, without the subtext or the culture behind it. An engineer who understood code before taking up the same tools is in a different position. To be accountable for a program is to be able to de-black-box what a model has produced in it. Somebody who cannot is a spokesperson who does not speak the language of those they represent, like a person who signs a contract they cannot read, and falls below the threshold imputation sets. The best a naive vibe coder can do with a black box is divide it into smaller black boxes.

The criteria are context, and so is everything else in this section, which leaves the duty that modularising context imposes: one home for each topic, and an explicit record of what supersedes what. Context does not all age the same way. Records, the decisions taken, the experiments run and the incidents written up, do not go stale, but their status changes when something supersedes them, and a superseded decision left where an agent can read it still looks like a decision. Descriptions, the specifications, rules and reference documents that say how things are, drift away from what they describe as it changes underneath them. Transients, the tickets, the feedback and an agent's memory of its last session, are meant to be used and let go, and they should expire. Treat all three as documentation and each fails in its own way, at greater cost than before, because an agent reads whatever it is given and treats it as current.

Contradiction is the worst of these failures, and review cannot be relied on to catch it. Two pieces of context that disagree will each be acted on by something, and the model in the middle has no reliable way to tell which was meant. So contradiction has to be prevented by structure: each topic has one home, and when something supersedes something else, the record says so. It is also how Toyota's learning loop reaches agents. A setback written up in a retrospective changes nothing an agent does. A setback traced to its cause, and fed into the criteria, the rules or the specification that allowed it, changes what every agent does next.

The same structure lets the CELC keep to the quality end of the range. Everything in this section moves the load of oversight: verification leaves the code and lands on the criteria and the gate, and the people at the gate carry the judgement Bainbridge foresaw. Managing that load needs no new idea. A limit on how much work may sit in review and verification at once, a limit on work in progress, already does it. Past the limit nothing new starts until something waiting has been judged, so the queue shows up as a queue, where everyone can see it, instead of as fatigue in the people at the end of the line. It is Toyota's cord, moved from the assembly line to the review column. Pulling it is a choice about the relations of production, about who carries the load of oversight and on what terms.

That is the lifecycle as far as an essay should take it. What it looks like in any one organisation is for that organisation to form.

## The wall at the edge of the firm

A lifecycle that lets software be shaped to how an organisation works has to say how far that shaping can go. For as long as engineering cycles were scarce, organisations could buy generic software off the shelf and bend the way their people worked to fit it, or spend heavily on internal tools that tended to become technical debt nobody could afford to pay down. Cheap generation softens that choice. Technically, at least, software can now be shaped to how a particular organisation, or a team inside one, actually works.

The obvious place to look for a limit is Conway's law. One reading says cheap generation makes the law cheap to obey: build software that copies how the organisation actually communicates, and rebuild it when that changes. Another says the law still binds, and that what it copies now includes the agents and whatever they have been given to read. Both treat the organisation as one thing and its software as another, as though every unit bound to its software in the same way. Dissected, that is preposterous. Organisations are heterogeneous, with functions that differ in what they do and who does it, and so is software. The answer is graduated, and a lot messier.

An organisation is aligned at least to the extent of sharing some goals, or it would be a list of actants with no influence on one another. That alignment is enrolment, in Callon's sense, and enrolment is never finished, which is how a business unit can fail in its objective while the wider business succeeds. Towards the core of an organisation more of its body is enrolled and its systems tend to be more homogeneous, because that is how the benefits of scale are captured. Towards the edge the systems can grow heterogeneous, all the way down to a single user with a personal dashboard and a knowledge base of their own, who can still enrol a dataset or a capability shared by a much wider unit. A young company shows the core forming: its founders start out holding several roles each, and as they hand the excess off, what lived in one or two heads has to move into the boundary objects between the roles.

Where the core ends and the edge begins depends on how the units are drawn, and the organisation chart is an arbitrary spokesperson for that. Organisations break into units and sub-units, teams and shifts, cliques and movements, formal groups and informal ones. The units nest, like a matryoshka doll, and they overlap, since one actant can belong to two groupings at once. So a system can obey Conway's law and not obey it at the same time, mirroring the unit it serves at one level and cutting across units at another.

None of this is exotic. The internet was built on what Jerome Saltzer, David Reed and David Clark named the end-to-end argument: a function that "can completely and correctly be implemented only with the knowledge and help of the application standing at the end points of the communication system" belongs at the ends (Saltzer et al., 1984). The network in the middle can then stay simple and common to everyone, while the variety lives at the edges. A federation has the same shape, with national law, states and councils, and so does a standard language, with a shared grammar at its core and dialects, jargon and idiolects at its edges. Each has a uniform core and heterogeneous edges, and its members live in both.

AI adds the capacity to manage a great deal more complexity in any one context. Retrieval-augmented generation is the plain case, a model mediating between a person and far more material than the person could hold. With that mediation the uniform core can grow larger and more complex, so it moves outward, and Conway's law becomes cheaper to obey for larger and less uniform units. It does not move for free. A company may add people and teams at a steady rate, but the relationships between them grow superlinearly, by the same arithmetic as for code, and a widening core inherits that.

The quickest way to find the limits is to take the stance too literally, shoving in more data and building more functions because a model will mediate them. Models do not use what they are given evenly. Nelson Liu and his colleagues found performance "often highest when relevant information occurs at the beginning or end of the input context", and degrading significantly when models "must access relevant information in the middle of long contexts, even for explicitly long-context models" (Liu et al., 2023). Chroma, a database vendor, tested 18 models in 2025 and reported that "their performance grows increasingly unreliable as input length grows" (Hong et al., 2025). A core widened by stuffing it is context left unmanaged, and an organisation that widens it that way invites a Cambrian explosion of complexity. Exactly where the limits fall, each organisation has to find for itself.

Somebody has to decide where each unit sits between the uniform core and the personal edge. That is a spokesperson's work, done by a leader at each level of the organisation: judging how far a unit should align to Conway's law, given its size and how uniform it is. The same spokesperson now has a second job, which is to manage how fast complexity is allowed to scale and how it is pruned, so that the organisation can hold what it builds.

The nesting has to stop somewhere, and I stop it at the wall, the boundary of the firm. That is a choice with a reason behind it: the boundary is where the shared goals stop, and shared goals are what align a unit at all. The firm also remains society's basic unit of economic organisation, and Ronald Coase gave the reason firms exist: directing production inside a firm can cost less than using the price mechanism (Coase, 1937). The wall is porous. The harness crosses it, since the interface a firm's people see may be set by a vendor outside the firm, and so does almost every dependency that is not worth the firm's while to make itself. Cheap generation moves Coase's boundary, since building and changing software inside the firm now costs less than it did, but it does not abolish the firm. My working claim is that customisation will track the units of self-organisation inside the firm, up to the firm's edge, and that AI allows a much closer alignment between software and how a firm actually organises itself than the SDLC ever did. Much closer is as far as I would put it.

## Four objections

The first objection is that the CELC is Patrick Debois's Context Development Lifecycle, the CDLC, under another name. Debois starts where I do: "if context is the new bottleneck, then we need a development lifecycle built around it". He treats context "as an engineering artifact (something we generate, evaluate, distribute, and observe)", and expects the CDLC to "reshape how we think about software development as profoundly as DevOps reshaped how we think about delivery and operations" (Debois, 2026). His account of context that goes stale and contradicts itself is close to mine. We differ on the object and on the claim. The CDLC's context is an artefact developed alongside code and in its service. It "needs the same engineering rigour we eventually gave to code: version control, testing, security, distribution, monitoring, and continuous improvement", and the post closes by asking: "You've invested in getting agents to write code. What have you invested in giving them the context to write it well?" (Debois, 2026). That is context as a material with a lifecycle. I also mean context as the medium that directs the software itself, the data and capabilities left once the how is stripped out. As for the claim, DevOps joined two halves of one lifecycle, and the lifecycle it joined carried on. A lifecycle modelled on DevOps extends the SDLC in the same way. The CELC claims succession, and so has more to prove.

The second objection is that all of this is one more abstraction layer, the next step after assembler, high-level languages and frameworks, and in one respect I concede it: context is the newest medium for directing computation. Martin Fowler accepts the scale of the change, putting it at "a similar degree as the change from assembler" to high-level languages, and then quotes Birgitta Böckeler: "we're moving *sideways* into non-determinism at the same time" (Böckeler, quoted in Fowler, 2025). Fortran kept in version control compiles to the same behaviour every time, and a prompt kept in version control does not reliably reproduce it. The earlier layers formed a ladder, each rung reducing reliably to the one below, and on every rung people still put the code down themselves. The SDLC organised the resources and processes that let people and teams of limited throughput put code down, so it carried on, one rung up, each time. On this rung we are no longer the ones putting the code down. I concede the layer and reject the conclusion drawn from it. The CELC is the SDLC abstracted: the lifecycle one layer up, organising the context through which the code, and everything else, is now produced.

The third objection is that the productivity is illusory, and the sceptics' data are its case. I take the same data as signs of complexity left unmanaged in its new location: structure that nobody maintains, costs arriving downstream as instability, and developers whose sense of their own speed parted from the measurement because part of the work had moved somewhere they were not looking. None of that proves the shift. It is consistent with it, and it says where the managing has to be done.

The fourth objection is the most serious. It comes from Rich Sutton's bitter lesson: that "general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin", and that "We have to learn the bitter lesson that building in how we think we think does not work in the long run" (Sutton, 2019). Rachel Laycock and her colleagues on Thoughtworks's Technology Advisory Board turned it on spec-driven development in their *Technology Radar* of November 2025: "We may be relearning a bitter lesson — that handcrafting detailed rules for AI ultimately doesn't scale" (Laycock et al., 2025, p.19). On the empirical point they are right. Handcrafted context does not scale. I have watched it fail on projects I have worked on, and the spec-driven trials say the same. But the CELC does not ask organisations to write their context by hand. Most of it is extracted, transformed and loaded from records the business already produces, and Sutton leaves room for exactly that when he writes that "we should build in only the meta-methods that can find and capture this arbitrary complexity" (Sutton, 2019). The bitter lesson holds for the handcrafted portion, which is the smaller one, and which a business curates by hand because it wants to govern it.

## The reorganisation lag

I end with the dynamo. David's factories waited four decades for their gains because the old plant had to wear out before replacing it paid. This transition lags for want of imagination: organisations formed around one set of tools find it hard to picture themselves rebuilt around another.

On David's evidence the gains came with the rebuild and not with the motor, and I expect the gains from agents to go to those who rebuild how they work around context. That means treating context as the unit that is produced, kept, corrected and superseded, moving verification off the code and onto criteria somebody answers for, and deciding, unit by unit, how closely software should follow the way each unit actually works. Running agents inside the old line shaft buys a new motor and keeps the old factory. The code arrives faster and piles up at review, and the gain stays out of sight.

This essay has argued that software is being redefined, that the lifecycle built for the old thing has to be succeeded, and that its complexity is now acted upon in context, where it also ages. An essay cannot say how any one organisation rebuilds around that. That is a question of method, and it needs a different kind of document, a follow-up whitepaper, which can describe the process of forming a CELC even though no finished one transfers. I can at least state the question: what does an organisation have to change, and in what order, to rebuild how it works around context instead of running agents on the old shaft? Until that is answered, the dynamos will keep turning, and the productivity statistics will keep missing them.
